How to Set Up a Home Office for Remote Work

A practical home office setup guide: choosing the spot, the desk and chair, lighting, internet, and the boundaries that keep work from taking over your home.

By Jordan Ellis · Work & Career · 12 min read · Last updated 3 November 2026

Working from home sounds comfortable until you spend a year hunched over a laptop at the kitchen table, lit by an overhead bulb, with your workday bleeding into your evening. A good home office is not about expensive gear. It is about four things in the right order: a dedicated spot, a body-friendly setup, decent light and sound, and boundaries that end the workday. This guide covers all four, with options for small spaces and tight budgets.

It helps to be honest about what you are solving for. If your back hurts, the chair and screen height matter most. If you are distracted, the spot and the household rules matter most. If you feel you never switch off, boundaries matter most. You do not need to fix everything in one weekend. Fix the worst problem first, live with it for a week, then fix the next one. A home office built in stages, around real problems, ends up better than one bought in a single shopping spree.

Step 1: Pick a spot — and keep it only for work

The single most effective change is giving work one place that is not also where you eat, sleep, or relax. A spare room is ideal, but a corner of a room works fine if you treat it as a room of its own. What matters is consistency: when you sit there, you are working; when you leave, you are not.

If space is genuinely tight, a few tricks help:

  • A folding screen, a bookshelf turned sideways, or even a curtain creates a visual boundary that your brain learns to respect.
  • Face a wall rather than the room, so movement behind you is not constantly pulling your attention.
  • Keep the work spot out of the bedroom if at all possible. Working where you sleep damages both the work and the sleep.

Measure before you buy anything. A desk that does not fit the corner, or a chair that cannot slide under it, is money wasted.

Also consider the less obvious factors: noise, temperature, and natural light. A quiet corner near a window beats a larger spot next to a television or a busy hallway. A spot that is freezing in winter or stifling in summer will drive you back to the sofa, however good the desk is. And think about video calls: what is behind you on camera? A plain wall or a tidy shelf looks professional; a bed, a laundry pile, or a busy doorway does not. You do not need a fake background — just a corner that looks calm on camera.

If you share a home, agree the spot with the people you live with before you set it up. A desk in a shared room works only if everyone understands that the corner is a workspace during work hours. That conversation, held once, prevents a hundred small interruptions later.

Step 2: The desk and chair — get these right first

If you buy only two things, buy these two, in this order: chair, then desk.

The chair. You sit in it for hours, so it matters more than anything else with a screen. You do not need a gaming chair or a designer chair. You need: adjustable seat height, lower-back support, and armrests that let your shoulders relax. When seated, your feet should rest flat on the floor, your knees at roughly a right angle, and the top of your screen at or just below eye level. If your current chair fails that test, a second-hand office chair from a reputable brand is often better than a new budget chair — office chairs are built for eight-hour days.

The desk. It needs to be deep enough (about 60–70 cm) that your screen is not right in front of your face, and sturdy enough not to wobble when you type. A simple tabletop on legs beats a tiny "computer desk" with a keyboard tray — keyboard trays force your wrists into awkward angles. If you already have a desk that is too high, a keyboard tray or a lower chair plus a footrest can fix the geometry without replacing it.

The screen. A laptop screen alone is too low and too small for full-time work. The cheapest serious upgrade is a laptop stand (or a stack of books, honestly) that raises the screen to eye level, plus a separate keyboard and mouse. If you can stretch to an external monitor, a 24-inch screen at eye level is the sweet spot for most people — bigger is nice, but 24 inches at the right height beats 32 inches on a pile of books.

Test the setup by sitting in it for a full workday before declaring it done. Small adjustments — raising the monitor by a book's thickness, moving the keyboard closer, adding a footrest if your feet dangle — make a disproportionate difference over eight hours. If your neck aches by afternoon, the screen is almost certainly too low. If your wrists ache, the keyboard is too high or too far away. If your lower back aches, the chair's lumbar support or your sitting posture needs attention, and a short walk every hour helps more than any cushion.

A standing desk is worth considering only after the seated setup is right. Standing all day brings its own problems, and a sit-stand desk used badly — standing for hours without moving — is no better than a bad chair. If you do go sit-stand, alternate in blocks, for example sitting for an hour then standing for twenty or thirty minutes, and wear supportive shoes rather than standing in socks on a hard floor.

Step 3: Light, sound, and internet

Light: Sit facing a window or with the window to your side, not behind you — light behind you turns you into a silhouette on video calls. For the darker months, a simple desk lamp aimed at the wall behind your screen gives soft, even light without glare. Avoid a single bright overhead bulb directly above the screen, which causes eye strain by afternoon.

Sound: You do not need a studio. A basic headset with a microphone makes you dramatically clearer on calls than a laptop microphone, and it signals to everyone else in the house that you are working. For focus, noise-cancelling headphones are worth it if your home is noisy; otherwise, a closed door and an agreement about quiet hours do most of the job for free.

Internet: Remote work lives or dies on connection quality. If your Wi-Fi is flaky at your desk, three fixes in order of cost: move closer to the router, try a different Wi-Fi channel in the router settings, or run an Ethernet cable (or a powerline adapter, which sends internet through your home's electrical wiring) to the desk. A wired connection is more stable than any Wi-Fi booster, and stability matters more than headline speed for video calls.

Test your video-call setup before an important meeting, not during it. Join a test call, check that your face is lit from the front, that your microphone is clear, and that background noise is not overwhelming. A two-minute test with a colleague or a friend saves the awkward first five minutes of a real meeting spent saying "can you hear me?" Also keep a backup plan for internet outages: a phone hotspot, a nearby place with Wi-Fi, or simply knowing how to tell your team you are offline. Outages happen; having a plan for them is part of a professional home setup.

Step 4: Organise the space so it stays usable

A home office decays into a dumping ground unless it has homes for things. Keep it simple:

  • One tray or drawer for papers that are actively in use; file or recycle the rest weekly.
  • Cables in one place: a cable tray under the desk or a few clips along the desk edge. Loose cables are not just ugly — they get yanked, which kills chargers and ports.
  • Everything you use daily within arm's reach; everything else in a cupboard. If you have to stand up to get a pen, the pen lives in the wrong place.

And put one thing in the space that is not work: a plant, a photo, a decent mug. A workspace that feels slightly personal is one you will actually sit in.

A weekly five-minute tidy, at the end of the last workday, prevents the slow drift into chaos. Clear the desk surface, file or recycle the week's papers, coil the cables, and put non-work items back where they belong. It is also a useful boundary ritual in itself: a tidy desk on Friday evening signals that the workweek is over, in a way that an untouched mess does not.

Think about storage for work materials separately from household storage. A small shelf or drawer that holds only work items — notebooks, chargers, documents — means you are not hunting through kitchen drawers for a work cable. Label it if you share the space, so other people know it is not general storage.

Step 5: Boundaries — the part most guides skip

The best desk in the world will not help if the workday never ends. Remote work's real problem is not comfort, it is bleed: the laptop stays open, messages arrive at dinner, and Monday starts on Sunday night. Set three rules and keep them:

  1. A start and end ritual. Open the laptop at a set time; close it at a set time. A short shutdown routine — write tomorrow's top three tasks, close all tabs, shut the laptop — tells your brain the day is over far better than willpower.
  2. A visible "at work" signal. A closed door, headphones on, or a simple sign. Housemates and family respect a signal they can see; they cannot respect one they have to guess.
  3. Notifications off after hours. Turn off work email and chat notifications on your phone after your end time. If something is genuinely urgent, people will call.

Boundaries also need an agreement with your team, not just with yourself. If your manager expects evening replies, a personal rule to switch off will fail. Raise it directly: agree response times, agree what counts as urgent, and agree how urgent matters reach you. Most teams are relieved to have that conversation, because everyone is quietly struggling with the same bleed. And if you work across time zones, be explicit about your working hours in your calendar and your messaging profile, so colleagues in other zones do not assume you are available around the clock.

Watch for the subtler form of bleed: working from the sofa "just for an hour" in the evening, or checking messages during a break and getting pulled back in. Those small breaches add up, and they teach your brain that the workday has no edge. Keeping work physically in the work spot, and the laptop closed after hours, is not rigid — it is what makes the time off actually restorative.

A sensible budget, in order

If you are starting from a kitchen table, spend in this order and stop when it feels right:

  1. Free: pick the spot, set the hours, fix the light.
  2. Under $50 / £40: laptop stand, separate keyboard and mouse, a desk lamp.
  3. $50–150 / £40–120: a decent second-hand office chair, a headset, a monitor arm or stand.
  4. $150+ / £120+: an external monitor, then a proper desk if the table is failing you.

Notice what is not on the list: fancy desk accessories, LED strips, and matching gear. They photograph well and add nothing to your work. Buy the chair before the aesthetic.

If your employer offers a home-office allowance or equipment, check what it covers before spending your own money. Some employers provide a monitor, a chair, or a stipend, and some have approved suppliers. Even if the allowance is small, it is worth claiming — and keep receipts for anything you buy yourself, in case it is claimable later or tax-relevant in your country. Do not assume; check your employer's policy and, if unsure, ask.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is buying gear before fixing the spot and the routine. A new monitor on a kitchen table, used at all hours, does not solve back pain or burnout. The second is copying someone else's setup wholesale: a tall person and a short person need different chair heights and monitor heights, and a setup that works in a spare room may not work in a shared flat. Adjust to your own body and room, not to a photo online.

The third mistake is neglecting breaks and movement. No chair, however good, replaces getting up. Stand, walk, stretch, and look away from the screen regularly — for example, a short break every hour, and a proper lunch break away from the desk. Your home office should make those breaks easy, not make you feel guilty for taking them. A water bottle at the desk, a comfortable place to sit elsewhere in the house, and a lunch routine away from the screen all help.

Questions people ask

Can I work from the bedroom if I have no other space? You can, but keep work as separate as possible within it: a corner desk facing the wall, a screen or curtain if you can manage it, and a rule that the laptop closes and leaves the desk at the end of the day. Avoid working from the bed itself — it harms posture, sleep, and focus all at once.

Is a second monitor worth it? For many roles, yes — especially if you regularly compare documents, keep a video call open while working, or use spreadsheets. Start with one good external monitor at eye level, and add a second only if you genuinely run out of space. Two badly placed monitors are worse than one well placed one.

How do I handle noise from family or neighbours? A headset with a microphone helps callers hear you clearly, and noise-cancelling headphones help you focus. Beyond gear, agree quiet hours, use a closed door where possible, and schedule important calls at quieter times when you can. If noise is constant, a white-noise app or background music through headphones can mask it without needing a silent house.

What should I do if my back still hurts after fixing the setup? First, check the basics again: feet flat, knees at a right angle, screen at eye level, and regular movement breaks. If pain persists, see a clinician rather than buying more cushions or gadgets. Persistent pain can have causes a desk setup cannot fix, and early advice is better than months of guessing.
