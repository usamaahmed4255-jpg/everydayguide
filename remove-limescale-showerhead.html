How to Remove Limescale From a Showerhead (Without Replacing It)

Weak shower pressure is often just limescale. Soak, scrub, and descale your showerhead and restore full flow in under an hour.

Why Your Shower Pressure Has Dropped

If your shower has gradually become weaker, sprayed unevenly, or started shooting water sideways from a few blocked holes, limescale is the most likely cause. Limescale is the chalky white deposit left when hard water evaporates or is heated. It builds up inside the showerhead, narrows the tiny spray holes, and forces water through fewer openings. The result feels like low pressure, even though your water supply has not changed.

Hard water areas are affected most, but limescale can build up anywhere over time, especially if the shower is used daily and never descaled. You may first notice white crust around the nozzles, a showerhead that feels heavier, or a spray pattern that has become patchy. Catching it early makes cleaning easier; a showerhead left for years can become so clogged that some holes stay blocked even after soaking, though most recover well with the method below.

Before you start, identify what type of showerhead you have. A fixed overhead head, a handheld head on a hose, and a showerhead with a filter all come apart slightly differently, but the cleaning principle is the same: soak in a descaling solution, clear the holes, rinse, and test. Check your manual if you have it, especially for heads with special coatings, electronic features, or built-in filters, because harsh cleaners can damage some finishes.

What You Need and What to Avoid

Gather a bowl or bucket large enough to submerge the showerhead, white vinegar or a citric-acid descaler, an old toothbrush, a pin or needle, a cloth, rubber gloves, and an adjustable spanner with a cloth to protect the fittings. A plastic bag and an elastic band are useful if you cannot remove the head, as explained later. You will also need a towel for drips and a spare washer, if you have one, in case the old washer is worn.

Avoid abrasive scourers, wire brushes, and harsh limescale removers not intended for bathroom fittings, especially on chrome, brushed metal, or coloured finishes. Abrasives scratch the surface, and once scratched, limescale grips even harder next time. Avoid boiling the showerhead in vinegar, which can damage seals and finishes. A warm or room-temperature soak is sufficient and safer.

Never mix descaling products. If you use vinegar, use vinegar alone. If you use a commercial descaler, follow its instructions exactly and do not add vinegar. Rinse thoroughly afterwards, because leftover descaler can irritate skin on the next shower. If your showerhead has rubber nozzles, be gentle with them; they are designed to flex, not to be scraped hard.

Remove the Showerhead Safely

Turn off the shower before you start. For a handheld head, unscrew it from the hose by hand if possible, turning anticlockwise. If it is tight, wrap a cloth around the fitting to protect the finish, then use an adjustable spanner gently. Hold the hose steady so you do not twist or kink it. For a fixed head, unscrew it from the shower arm in the same way, supporting the arm so it does not bend.

As you unscrew the head, note the order of parts: there is usually a washer or rubber seal inside the fitting, and sometimes a small filter mesh. Place these parts in a small bowl so they do not get lost. Take a photo of how they fit together before you separate them. If the washer looks cracked, flattened, or torn, replace it when you reassemble; a worn washer is a common cause of drips after cleaning, and a new washer costs very little.

If the head will not unscrew, do not force it with excessive leverage, because you can damage the shower arm or hose fitting. Try a little penetrating oil around the thread, wait a few minutes, and try again with the cloth and spanner. If it still will not move, use the bag method below, which cleans the head without removing it. Forcing a seized fitting can turn a simple clean into a plumbing repair.

Soak to Dissolve the Limescale

Place the removed showerhead in a bowl or bucket, nozzles facing down if possible, and cover it with your descaling solution. The simplest home solution is equal parts white vinegar and water — for example, one cup of vinegar to one cup of water as an illustrative mix. Submerge the head fully, or at least up to the nozzles and internal passages. Leave it to soak for 30 minutes to an hour for moderate build-up. For heavy build-up, you can extend the soak to two hours, checking occasionally.

If you prefer citric acid, dissolve it in warm water following the packet instructions, and soak in the same way. Citric acid is effective and has less smell than vinegar, which some people prefer. Whichever solution you use, do not soak for far longer than recommended, especially on plated finishes, because prolonged acid exposure can dull the finish. Set a timer so you do not forget the head soaking overnight by accident.

While the head soaks, clean the filter mesh and washer separately in the same solution for a few minutes, then rinse them. Wipe the shower arm or hose end with a damp cloth to remove surface limescale, being careful not to let debris fall into the open pipe. If you see heavy scale on the arm thread, a short wipe with vinegar on a cloth, followed by a clean-water wipe, is enough.

As a worked example, consider a handheld head that sprays in a lopsided pattern with several holes blocked. After a one-hour vinegar soak, the white crust around the nozzles will have softened visibly, and many holes will clear with gentle brushing alone. That softening is the sign the soak has worked; you should not need to scrape hard to finish the job.

Scrub, Clear the Holes, and Rinse

After soaking, remove the head from the solution and scrub the nozzle face with an old toothbrush, using clean water. Work in small circles, paying attention to the edges of each nozzle where scale collects. For rubber nozzles, flex them gently with your fingers or the brush; this often pops loosened scale out of the holes. Avoid pushing a pin into every hole as a first step, because you can damage the nozzle if you force it. Brushing and flexing should clear most holes.

For holes that remain blocked after brushing, use a pin or needle very gently, just to clear the opening, not to ream it wider. Insert the pin straight, wiggle slightly, and withdraw. Do not force it, and do not use a drill or a thick wire. After clearing stubborn holes, rinse the head thoroughly under running water, shaking it gently to flush loosened particles out through the inlet as well as the nozzles.

Rinse the filter mesh and washer too, and check them for damage. Reassemble the head in the reverse order of removal, using your photo as a guide. Fit the washer correctly — a washer that sits crooked will leak. Screw the head back onto the hose or arm by hand first, to avoid cross-threading, and then tighten gently with the cloth and spanner if needed. Do not overtighten; hand-tight plus a small final turn is usually enough, especially with a new washer.

If You Cannot Remove the Head: The Bag Method

Some fixed showerheads are difficult to remove, or you may be renting and do not want to risk damaging the fitting. In that case, use the bag method. Fill a sturdy plastic bag with enough vinegar-and-water solution to submerge the nozzle face, place the bag over the head, and secure it tightly around the shower arm with an elastic band or tape. Make sure the nozzles are fully submerged and the bag will not slip off.

Leave the bag in place for 30 minutes to an hour, as with a bowl soak. Then remove the bag carefully — the solution will drip — scrub the nozzle face with a toothbrush, flex rubber nozzles gently, and run the shower for a minute to flush loosened scale. Wipe the head dry and test the spray pattern. The bag method is less thorough than a full soak, because the internal passages do not get the same circulation, but it works well for moderate build-up and for heads that cannot be removed.

If the bag method does not fully restore flow, repeat it once, or try removing the head if you feel confident. Do not leave the bag on overnight, for the same finish-protection reason as a long soak. If the head is very heavily scaled and cannot be removed, a longer or repeated bag soak may still help, but at some point a replacement head becomes more practical than repeated cleaning — that point is usually when many holes remain blocked after two thorough soaks and gentle clearing.

Test the Flow and Check for Leaks

Turn the shower on slowly and check the spray pattern. It should be even, with water coming from most or all holes, and no strong sideways jets. Let it run for a minute to flush any remaining particles. If a few holes are still blocked, turn the shower off, brush those nozzles again, and test once more. Do not be surprised if a little dislodged scale comes out on the first run; that is normal after descaling.

Check the fitting where the head joins the hose or arm for drips. A slow drip after reassembly usually means the washer is worn, misaligned, or the fitting is not quite tight enough. Turn the shower off, check the washer position, and tighten gently. If dripping persists with a new washer and correct alignment, the thread or fitting itself may be worn, and a replacement washer or head may be needed.

Compare the flow before and after by timing how long it takes to fill a bucket, if you want a simple check. As an example only, if a bucket took an illustrative 60 seconds to fill before cleaning and 40 seconds after, that is a clear improvement, though your own times will vary with your water supply. The real test is everyday use: an even spray, normal pressure, and no sideways jets over the next few showers.

Prevent Limescale From Building Up Again

Prevention is easier than a heavy descale. After each shower, if your head has rubber nozzles, rub them quickly with your fingers or a cloth to dislodge early scale before it hardens. That takes seconds and significantly slows build-up. Wipe the head dry occasionally, especially in hard-water areas, so water does not evaporate and leave deposits on the surface.

Descale routinely, before the spray becomes patchy. Every three to six months is a good interval for hard-water areas, and every six to twelve months for softer water. Mark it in your calendar alongside other household tasks, so it does not get forgotten until the shower is badly blocked. A routine 30-minute soak is far easier than a two-hour rescue clean.

Common Mistakes That Damage the Finish or Waste Effort

The most common mistake is scrubbing with an abrasive pad or a metal brush, which scratches chrome and makes future scale stick worse. Use a soft brush and a soak to do the work, not force. Another mistake is using undiluted vinegar or a very strong descaler for a long soak, thinking stronger is better. Stronger acid for longer does not clean proportionally faster, and it risks dulling finishes and damaging seals. Follow the dilution and time guidance above.

Forcing a seized head is another costly mistake. If it will not unscrew with gentle leverage, use the bag method or seek help, rather than bending the shower arm. Similarly, overtightening on reassembly can crack plastic fittings or crush the washer, causing leaks. Hand-tight plus a gentle final turn is the rule.

Finally, do not ignore a leak after cleaning. A drip that seems minor can waste water and damage the fitting over time. Check the washer, check alignment, and replace the washer if needed. A clean showerhead that leaks is not a finished job.

If This Does Not Work, Try This

If flow is still weak after a thorough soak, brush, and rinse, check whether the problem is really the showerhead. Remove the head and run the shower without it, briefly, into a bucket. If flow without the head is also weak, the problem is upstream: the hose, the shower valve, the water pressure, or a blocked filter in the shower unit. Clean or replace the hose if it is kinked or internally scaled, and check any inlet filter in the shower valve following the manual.

If flow without the head is strong but weak with the head, the head itself is still blocked internally or its filter is spent. Repeat the soak once, clear holes gently, and check for a replaceable filter. If many holes remain blocked after two thorough attempts, or the head is cracked, corroded, or very old, replacing the head is reasonable. A standard replacement head is widely available and usually fits the same hose or arm, but check the thread size and fitting type before buying.

If water pressure is low throughout the house, not just in the shower, the issue is broader than limescale. Check whether a stopcock is partly closed, whether neighbours have the same issue, or whether your supplier reports low pressure. Do not adjust mains plumbing yourself unless you are competent to do so; contact a plumber or your water supplier for a whole-house pressure problem.

Questions People Ask

How often should I descale a showerhead?

In hard-water areas, every three to six months is a good routine. In softer water, every six to twelve months is usually enough. Descale sooner if you notice white crust, uneven spray, or sideways jets, rather than waiting for flow to become seriously restricted.

Is vinegar safe for all showerheads?

White vinegar diluted with water is safe for most standard chrome and plastic heads for a 30- to 60-minute soak, but check your manual for special finishes, coatings, or electronic heads. Do not use undiluted vinegar for a long soak, do not boil the head in vinegar, and rinse thoroughly afterwards. If in doubt, use a descaler recommended by the head's maker.

Can I use a commercial descaler instead of vinegar?

Yes, provided it is suitable for bathroom fittings and you follow its instructions exactly. Do not mix it with vinegar or other cleaners, do not exceed the recommended soak time, and rinse thoroughly. Commercial descalers can be convenient and less smelly than vinegar, but they are not automatically gentler — follow the packet.

Why is my shower still weak after descaling?

Either the head is still internally blocked, its filter is spent, the hose is kinked or scaled, or the problem is upstream pressure or valve flow. Test flow without the head to narrow it down, as described above. If flow without the head is strong, focus on the head or its filter; if it is weak too, look upstream.

Should I just replace the showerhead instead of cleaning it?

Cleaning is worth trying first, because it takes under an hour and costs very little, and most heads recover fully. Replace the head if it remains badly blocked after two thorough cleans, if it is cracked or corroded, if the fitting is damaged, or if its filter cannot be replaced and is spent. A replacement is a practical choice at that point, not a failure of cleaning.
