How to Remove Common Stains From Clothes

Coffee, red wine, grease, blood, ink — each stain needs a different treatment. A stain-by-stain guide with what to use, what to avoid, and when not to use hot water.

The biggest stain mistake is treating every stain the same way. Hot water sets some stains permanently. Bleach ruins some fabrics. And the all-purpose "stain remover" you spray on everything works brilliantly on some marks and does nothing on others. Stain removal is mostly about matching the treatment to the stain — and acting before the stain dries and before the garment goes through a hot dryer, which can set a stain for good. Here is the stain-by-stain guide.

It helps to think of stains in families, because the treatment follows the family. Water-soluble stains (coffee, tea, most drinks) respond to water and detergent. Grease stains need something that cuts fat. Protein stains (blood, sweat, dairy, egg) must never meet hot water first. Dye stains (grass, ink, red wine) need a solvent or a soak that lifts colour without spreading it. If you can identify the family, you can usually choose a safe first step even if you are not sure of the exact stain.

The three rules that apply to every stain

Rule 1: Act fast, but do not panic-scrub. Blot — press a clean cloth onto the stain to lift liquid — instead of rubbing, which pushes the stain deeper into the fibres and spreads it. Work from the outside of the stain inward, so you are not enlarging it.

Rule 2: Cold water first, unless you know better. Cold water is safe for almost everything. Hot water sets protein stains (blood, sweat, dairy, egg) by effectively cooking them into the fabric. If you are unsure what a stain is, start cold.

Rule 3: Never put a stained garment in the dryer until the stain is gone. Heat sets stains. Check after washing, while the garment is still damp: if the mark is still there, treat again and rewash. Once it has been through a hot dryer, many stains become permanent.

Always test any treatment on a hidden spot first (inside hem, inside seam) and wait a few minutes, especially on coloured or delicate fabrics. And check the care label: "dry clean only" means what it says — take it to a cleaner and point out the stain rather than experimenting.

Keep a small stain kit ready, so you are not hunting for supplies while a stain dries. A basic kit is: clean white cloths, liquid laundry detergent, plain dish soap, baking soda, white vinegar, rubbing alcohol, and oxygen-based stain remover. That covers almost every stain in this guide. Store them together, away from children and pets, and label anything decanted into an unmarked bottle.

Coffee and tea

Fresh coffee is mostly water-soluble, so speed matters more than chemistry. Blot up as much as possible, then flush the stain from the back with cold water — hold the fabric under a running tap with the stained side facing down, so the water pushes the coffee out rather than through. Rub in a small amount of liquid laundry detergent or dish soap, let it sit for five minutes, rinse, and wash normally.

For dried coffee, soak in cold water with a scoop of oxygen-based stain remover (sodium percarbonate — sold as OxiClean and store brands) for 30 minutes to a few hours before washing. Do not use chlorine bleach on coloured fabric; it will take the colour with the stain.

Tea follows the same treatment, with one extra note: strong black tea can leave a tannin mark that looks faint after washing but darkens with heat. That is another reason to check before drying. If a faint tea shadow remains after washing, soak again in oxygen stain remover before drying, rather than hoping the dryer will finish the job — it will do the opposite.

If coffee contains milk, treat it as both a water-soluble and a protein stain: cold water first, always. Hot water on milky coffee can set the milk protein, leaving a mark even after the coffee colour is gone. The same applies to hot chocolate and milky tea.

Red wine

Red wine feels like an emergency, but it is very treatable if you move quickly. Blot — do not rub — then cover the stain generously with table salt or baking soda, which draws the liquid out as it dries. Leave it until dry, brush off the residue, then flush with cold water and wash with detergent.

Two things that are myths: white wine does not neutralise red wine (it just adds more liquid), and pouring boiling water through the stain is risky on delicate fabrics. Cold water, salt, and detergent handle most red wine stains without drama. For a dried red wine stain, soak in cold water with oxygen stain remover for several hours before washing, and check before drying.

If the stain is on a white garment, oxygen stain remover is your best friend, used as a soak rather than as a quick spray. Give it time — several hours for a dried stain — and keep the garment fully submerged. If the stain is on a coloured garment, test the oxygen remover on a hidden spot first, and do not use chlorine bleach. A red wine stain that survives two careful treatments is a candidate for a professional cleaner, especially on silk, wool, or a garment you cannot replace.

Grease and cooking oil

Grease repels water, so water alone does nothing — you need something that cuts fat. Dish soap is made for exactly this. Blot off excess oil with a paper towel, apply a drop of plain dish soap directly to the stain, and work it in gently with your fingers or a soft toothbrush. Let it sit for 10–15 minutes, rinse with the warmest water the care label allows, and wash normally.

For heavy grease (car oil, butter left overnight), sprinkle cornstarch or talcum powder on the stain first and leave it for 30 minutes to absorb the oil, brush it off, then do the dish soap step. Repeat if a shadow remains — grease often needs two rounds. Do not use hot water on a stain you have not treated yet; heat can set grease into synthetic fabrics.

The most common grease mistake is washing first and checking later. An untreated grease stain that goes through a wash, and especially a dryer, can become a permanent dark shadow that no amount of dish soap will lift afterwards. Always treat grease before washing, and check while the garment is still damp. If a shadow remains, repeat the dish soap step and wash again — grease is one of the stains where a second round is normal, not a sign of failure.

Blood

Blood is a protein stain, so the cold-water rule is absolute: never hot water first. Rinse immediately under cold running water, rubbing gently, and most fresh blood comes out with water alone. For dried blood, soak in cold salt water (a tablespoon of salt per litre) for 30 minutes to a few hours, then wash with detergent. Hydrogen peroxide (3%, the pharmacy kind) works on white cotton, but it can bleach colours — test it on a hidden spot first, and never use it on wool or silk.

Dried blood is patient work. Do not be tempted to speed it up with hot water, which will set the protein and make the stain far harder to remove. Soak, check, rinse, and soak again if needed. For blood on a mattress or upholstery, where soaking is not possible, dab with cold water and a cloth, working from the outside in, and blot dry between rounds rather than saturating the material.

Ink (ballpoint pen)

Ink is the stain most people give up on too early. For ballpoint ink, place the stained area face-down on a clean white cloth or paper towel, then dab rubbing alcohol (isopropyl alcohol) or hand sanitiser onto the back of the stain. The ink transfers into the cloth underneath. Move the cloth to a clean spot frequently, so you are not pressing the ink back in. Repeat until no more ink transfers, then rinse and wash normally.

Do not use hairspray — modern hairsprays mostly lack the alcohol that made the old trick work. And do not use hot water before the ink is out; heat sets ink fast. Gel pen and fountain pen ink are harder and may need a dedicated ink remover or a professional cleaner; if the garment matters, take it in rather than experimenting.

Patience is the key with ink. It may take many rounds of dabbing, each transferring a little more ink, before the stain visibly fades. Changing the cloth underneath frequently is essential — otherwise you are simply moving ink from the garment into a saturated cloth and back again. Work in a ventilated area when using rubbing alcohol, and keep it away from flames.

Sweat and yellow underarm marks

Yellow marks are usually a combination of sweat and antiperspirant, and they build up gradually. Make a paste of baking soda and a little water, spread it on the mark, and leave it for 30 minutes before washing. For stubborn marks, soak in a solution of oxygen stain remover and cool water for a few hours. White vinegar in the rinse (half a cup in the machine's rinse cycle) helps with odour and residue, but it is not a substitute for treating the mark itself.

Prevention is easier than cure here: let antiperspirant dry fully before dressing, and wash shirts promptly rather than leaving sweaty garments in a pile for days.

Yellow marks on white shirts are often mistaken for a simple sweat stain, when the real culprit is antiperspirant build-up combined with body oils. That is why detergent alone often fails: the mark is layered, and it needs a paste or soak that breaks down the build-up before washing. If a mark has been through many hot dryer cycles, it may be permanent — another reason to treat early and check before drying.

Chocolate, tomato sauce, and grass

These three show up constantly and each has one trick:

  • Chocolate: Scrape off excess, flush with cold water, apply dish soap or detergent, rinse, wash. Chocolate is fat plus pigment — the dish soap handles the fat, the wash handles the rest.
  • Tomato sauce: Scrape off excess without spreading it, flush from the back with cold water, apply dish soap, rinse, then wash. Sunlight fades remaining tomato discolouration on white cotton — drying in the sun is a legitimate last step, not an old wives' tale.
  • Grass: Grass stains are dye stains. Apply white vinegar or rubbing alcohol, let it sit for 30 minutes, rinse, then wash with detergent. Do not use hot water first.

For chocolate, remember the milk rule: if it is milk chocolate, cold water first, because of the milk protein. For tomato sauce on white fabric, sunlight is genuinely useful as a final step, but only after washing — drying a still-stained garment in the sun does not replace treatment, it just fades what is left after treatment. For grass, work on the stain before it dries if you can; dried grass dye is harder to lift, and may need a longer soak.

If this does not work, try this

If a stain survives one careful treatment, do not escalate to the strongest chemical you own. Repeat the correct treatment once, with a longer soak time, and check again. Most stains that survive a first attempt yield to a second, longer attempt with the same method. Escalating to bleach on a coloured garment, or to hot water on a protein stain, usually makes things worse, not better.

If two attempts with the right method have not shifted a stain, stop and reassess: is the fabric suitable for stronger treatment, is the care label being respected, and is the garment worth a professional clean? A cleaner who knows what the stain is and what you have already tried has a far better chance than a third home attempt with a random product. Tell them both facts — stain and prior treatments — when you hand it over.

When to stop and call a cleaner

Take the garment to a professional if: the care label says dry clean only; the fabric is silk, wool, leather, or suede; the stain is large, old, and already heat-set; or your treatments have not shifted it after two attempts. Tell the cleaner what the stain is and what you have already tried — that information genuinely helps them, and guessing wrong wastes their time and your money.

Most stains are not emergencies. Cold water, the right soap, and patience before the dryer will save the large majority of clothes that people currently throw away. The stain is rarely the disaster; the hot dryer cycle afterwards is.

Questions people ask

Can I use bleach on a stain? Only on white, bleach-safe fabric, and only after checking the care label. Chlorine bleach will damage colours, wool, and silk, and it can weaken fabric if overused. Oxygen-based stain remover is the safer default for most stains, including on colours, provided you test it first.

What should I do if I do not know what the stain is? Start cold, blot, and use a small amount of liquid detergent. Cold water and detergent are safe for most unknown stains, and they will not set a protein stain the way hot water would. If the stain does not shift, try to narrow it down — greasy feel, colour, smell, location — before choosing a stronger treatment.

Is it too late if the stain has already dried? Usually not. Dried stains need longer soaking, but most respond to the same treatments given more time. The exception is a stain that has been through a hot dryer, which may be permanently set. Even then, a professional cleaner is worth asking before giving up on a garment you value.

Should I treat a stain on a dry-clean-only garment myself? No, beyond blotting up excess liquid. Home treatments, especially water-based ones, can damage dry-clean-only fabrics or leave water marks. Take it to a cleaner promptly, and point out the stain so they can treat it before general cleaning.
