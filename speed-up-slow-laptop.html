How to Speed Up a Slow Laptop (Windows and Mac)

A slow laptop usually has one or two fixable causes. Learn how to find the culprit, clear startup clutter, free disk space, and speed up Windows and Mac.

A slow laptop rarely means a dying laptop. In most cases, one or two things are hogging its resources: too many programs launching at startup, a nearly full hard drive, or a browser carrying fifty open tabs. This guide finds the actual culprit first, then fixes it — in order, from the quick wins to the one hardware upgrade that is genuinely worth the money.

The temptation when a laptop slows down is to change everything at once: install a cleaner app, tweak settings you found in a forum, delete files in a panic. That approach usually makes things worse, because you never learn what was actually wrong, and you can undo a fix that was working. Work in order instead. Diagnose, fix the biggest cause, restart, and check whether it helped before moving on. Most laptops improve noticeably after the first two or three steps, and you may never need the later ones.

First, find what is actually slowing it down

Guessing wastes time. Spend two minutes looking at the numbers before changing anything.

On Windows: Press Ctrl + Shift + Esc to open Task Manager. Click "More details" if you see a compact view, then look at the Processes tab. Click the CPU, Memory, and Disk column headers, one at a time, to sort by each. Anything sitting above 90% for a long stretch is your problem. A browser using 3 GB of memory with forty tabs open is a normal finding, not a mystery.

On Mac: Open Spotlight (Cmd + Space), type "Activity Monitor", and press Enter. The CPU, Memory, and Energy tabs tell the same story. Click "Memory" and look at the "Memory Pressure" graph at the bottom: green is fine, yellow means strain, red means the machine is genuinely struggling.

Write down the top two or three offenders. Everything below targets those, not random settings. It helps to note when the slowness happens, too. A laptop that is slow only for the first five minutes after switching on probably has a startup problem. One that is slow only in the browser has a browser problem. One that is slow all the time, even with nothing open, may have a full drive, a failing drive, or a background process stuck in a loop. That one observation can save you an hour of fixing the wrong thing.

If a single program is using most of the CPU or memory and you do not recognise it, search its exact name on the web before ending it. Some unfamiliar names are normal system processes. Ending a genuine system process can crash the machine, while ending a runaway updater or a forgotten video editor is perfectly safe. When in doubt, restart the laptop first and see whether the same process comes back and misbehaves again. A one-off spike after an update is not the same as a persistent problem.

Clear the startup crowd

Many programs set themselves to launch every time the computer starts, then sit in the background all day. You probably need three or four of them. The rest can wait until you open them.

On Windows: In Task Manager, open the Startup tab. Look at the "Startup impact" column. Right-click and disable anything marked High that you do not need within seconds of logging in — chat apps, game launchers, update helpers, and printer utilities are the usual suspects. Disabling a startup entry does not uninstall the program; it just stops it launching uninvited. Keep your antivirus and your cloud sync (OneDrive, Dropbox) enabled if you rely on them daily.

On Mac: Go to System Settings → General → Login Items. Remove anything you do not recognise or do not need at login. Also check "Allow in the Background" on the same screen and switch off helpers for apps you rarely use.

Restart after this step. Startup changes only take full effect after a reboot, and the difference is often obvious immediately. If you disable something and later realise you miss it launching automatically, you can turn it back on in the same place — nothing here is permanent, which is why it is a safe first fix.

A common mistake is disabling everything, including security software and the tool that keeps your password manager or backup running. Be selective. Ask of each entry: do I need this running before I have even opened an app? If the answer is no, disable it. Your chat app will still work when you click it; it just will not be sitting in memory all day waiting.

Free up disk space — aim for 15–20% free

A drive that is 95% full slows everything down, because the system has nowhere to put temporary files. You do not need to delete your photos. Start with the easy wins:

On Windows: Go to Settings → System → Storage. Turn on Storage Sense, which automatically clears temporary files and the Recycle Bin. Then click "Temporary files" and remove what it suggests. Next, sort your Downloads folder by size and delete installers and files you no longer need — old .exe and .dmg installers are often gigabytes of dead weight.

On Mac: Click the Apple menu → About This Mac → More Info → Storage Settings. macOS will offer to store files in iCloud, empty the Trash automatically, and reduce clutter. Review its recommendations before accepting them, especially anything involving moving your only copy of a file to the cloud.

What not to delete: anything in the Windows folder, anything in System folders on a Mac, and files you cannot identify. If you are unsure, leave it. Deleting the wrong system file creates a much bigger problem than a slow laptop.

Work through the large files methodically rather than deleting at random. Sort a folder by size, largest first, and you will often find a handful of files doing most of the damage: old video exports, duplicate downloads, a game you finished months ago, phone backups kept long after you changed phones. Moving photos and videos to an external drive or a cloud service you already pay for is a better move than deleting them, provided you check afterwards that the copies actually open before you remove the originals. One verified copy in a second place is a backup; one copy moved in a hurry, unchecked, is a risk.

If freeing space is hard because the drive itself is small, check for the hidden space-eaters: multiple user accounts with their own downloads, a mail app storing years of attachments locally, or a messaging app keeping every photo and video ever received. Clearing an app's cache from inside the app is usually safer than hunting through its folders by hand.

Tame the browser

For most people, the browser is the slowest program on the machine — not because the browser is bad, but because of how it is used. Each open tab uses memory, and each extension adds a little more.

  • Close tabs you are not actively reading. If you want to keep something, bookmark it (Ctrl + D or Cmd + D) instead of leaving the tab open "just in case".
  • Audit your extensions: in Chrome, go to chrome://extensions; in Edge, edge://extensions; in Safari, Settings → Extensions. Remove anything you do not remember installing and anything you have not used in a month.
  • In Chrome, press Shift + Esc to open the browser's own task manager. It shows which tab or extension is eating memory, which is often a surprise — a single news site with auto-playing video can use more than everything else combined.

Extensions deserve a second look because they run on every page you visit. A coupon finder, a screen recorder, and a PDF tool you installed for one task in 2023 can each add a small delay to every page load. Remove first, and only reinstall an extension when you actually need it again. The same applies to toolbars and search helpers that arrived bundled with another download — if you did not choose it, remove it.

If one particular site is always slow while everything else is fine, the problem may be that site, not your laptop. Test it in a private or incognito window with extensions disabled. If it speeds up, an extension or a stored cookie is the cause. Clearing cookies for that one site, rather than wiping all browsing data, keeps you signed in everywhere else.

Restart properly, and install updates

Sleep mode is not a restart. A laptop that has not been properly restarted in weeks accumulates stuck processes and memory leaks. Restart at least once a week. It takes two minutes and fixes a surprising number of "my laptop is slow" complaints on its own.

While you are at it, install pending system updates. On Windows: Settings → Windows Update. On Mac: System Settings → General → Software Update. Updates sometimes include performance fixes, and running an old version with known bugs is a needless handicap.

Check the basics people overlook

Before spending money, rule out three simple causes. First, heat: a laptop that is hot to the touch, with fans running loudly, will slow itself down deliberately to avoid damage. Use it on a hard, flat surface rather than a bed or a lap, and check that the vents are not blocked by dust or a case. Compressed air, used in short bursts with the machine off, can clear a dusty vent, but do not poke anything into the fan.

Second, power settings: a laptop on a battery-saving mode, or plugged into a weak charger, may run at reduced speed. On Windows, check the power mode in Settings → System → Power; on a Mac, check Energy settings. If the laptop is only slow when unplugged, that is expected behaviour, not a fault.

Third, background syncing: a cloud service uploading a large video, a photo library syncing after a holiday, or a backup running for the first time can saturate the disk and the network for hours. Check your cloud and backup apps before concluding the machine itself is slow. Pausing a sync temporarily is a useful diagnostic — if speed returns, you have found the cause, and you can let the sync finish overnight instead.

The one hardware upgrade worth the money

If your laptop still has a traditional spinning hard drive (HDD) rather than a solid-state drive (SSD), that is very likely the bottleneck — and it is the one upgrade that transforms an old machine. An SSD makes startup, app launches, and file searches dramatically faster, and a standard SATA SSD typically costs in the range of $30–60 for a common size. Fitting one is a straightforward job for a repair shop if you are not comfortable opening the machine, and it is worth getting a quote before buying a whole new laptop.

The other hardware question is memory (RAM). 4 GB is tight for modern use; 8 GB is comfortable for everyday work. If Activity Monitor or Task Manager shows memory pressure constantly in the red even after closing programs, a RAM upgrade — if your model allows it — is the second upgrade worth considering. Many thin modern laptops have soldered RAM that cannot be upgraded, so check your model's specs before spending anything.

What not to do

  • Do not install "PC cleaner" or "speed booster" apps. Most do nothing useful, some install ads or worse, and Windows and macOS already include the cleanup tools you need.
  • Do not disable your antivirus to gain speed. The risk is not worth the small gain, and modern antivirus is rarely the cause.
  • Do not edit the Windows Registry or delete system files on the advice of a random forum post. That is how a slow laptop becomes a broken laptop.

If this does not work, try this

Work through this short troubleshooting sequence before giving up. If the laptop is slow only in one app, reinstall that app rather than rebuilding the whole system — a corrupted install is a common, fixable cause. If it is slow after a recent change, undo that change first: remove the new program, extension, or setting, restart, and test. If it is slow everywhere and nothing above helped, try a fresh user account on the same machine. If the new account is fast, your own profile — its startup items, its browser data, its background apps — is the problem, and migrating to a clean profile may be quicker than hunting further.

If the drive makes clicking or grinding noises, if files disappear or refuse to open, or if the laptop shuts down without warning, stop troubleshooting and back up immediately, then take it to a repair shop. Those are signs of a failing drive or a battery problem, not settings to tweak. Similarly, a battery that is swelling — a trackpad that feels raised, a case that no longer sits flat — is a safety issue: do not charge it or press on it, and get it looked at promptly.

The ten-minute checklist

  1. Check Task Manager or Activity Monitor — find the top offender.
  2. Disable unneeded startup programs, then restart.
  3. Free space until at least 15–20% of the drive is empty.
  4. Close unused tabs and remove unused browser extensions.
  5. Install pending updates.
  6. If it is still slow and has an old HDD, price an SSD upgrade before replacing the machine.

Work through that list in order and most laptops feel noticeably faster by step four. If nothing helps, the problem may be a failing drive or battery swelling — both are repair-shop jobs, not settings to tweak.

Questions people ask

Why is my laptop slow only after I switch it on? That pattern usually points to startup programs. Everything launching at once competes for the disk and memory, so the first few minutes feel dreadful even though the machine is fine afterwards. Clear the startup list, restart, and time the boot again before looking elsewhere.

Will more RAM always make my laptop faster? Not always. Extra RAM helps when memory pressure is the bottleneck — constant yellow or red pressure, or heavy swapping to disk. If the disk is full, the drive is an old HDD, or one app is misbehaving, RAM alone will not fix it. Diagnose first, then buy the upgrade that matches the diagnosis.

Is it worth reinstalling the operating system? Sometimes, as a last resort for a machine clogged with years of software, but it is a big job: back up everything, reinstall, then reinstall your apps and restore your files. Try the steps in this guide first. A reinstall that you did not need wastes a weekend; a reinstall after a proper diagnosis is a clean, deliberate fix.

How often should I restart? Once a week is a sensible habit for most people. If you leave the laptop asleep for weeks, stuck processes and memory leaks accumulate. A weekly restart is free, takes two minutes, and prevents a whole class of vague slowness.
