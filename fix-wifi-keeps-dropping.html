How to Fix Wi-Fi That Keeps Dropping (Router, Device, and Signal Fixes)

Wi-Fi that drops every few minutes usually has one fixable cause. Work through router, device, and signal checks in order to find it.

What "Dropping" Actually Means

Wi-Fi that keeps dropping does not always mean the same thing, and naming the problem correctly saves a lot of wasted effort. Sometimes the connection disappears completely and your device shows "No Internet" or disconnects from the network name. Sometimes you stay connected to Wi-Fi but pages stop loading, video calls freeze, and games lag out. Sometimes only one device drops while everything else works fine. Each pattern points to a different cause.

Start by watching what happens for a few minutes. Does the Wi-Fi icon vanish, or does it stay but nothing loads? Does it happen on every device in the house, or just your laptop or phone? Does it happen at a particular time, in a particular room, or when a particular appliance runs? Write down the answers before you change anything. That short note becomes your test later: after each fix, you can check whether the same pattern is still happening.

A useful first test is to compare wired and wireless. If you can plug a laptop into the router with an Ethernet cable, even temporarily, and the wired connection stays stable while Wi-Fi drops, the problem is almost certainly wireless — signal, interference, or the router's Wi-Fi radio. If the wired connection drops too, the problem is more likely your internet line, your modem, or your internet provider. That distinction alone can save you an hour of tweaking Wi-Fi settings that were never the issue.

Check Whether It Is One Device or Every Device

Before touching the router, check a second device. Open a video or a speed test on your phone while your laptop is dropping, or ask someone else in the household whether their connection is dropping at the same moment. If every device drops together, focus on the router, the modem, and the line coming into your home. If only one device drops, focus on that device: its Wi-Fi adapter, its software, its power settings, and its distance from the router.

Also check whether the drop is really Wi-Fi or really the internet. When the drop happens, look at the router's lights. Most routers have a light for internet or WAN. If that light goes red or off during a drop, the router itself has lost its connection to your provider. If the internet light stays green while your device loses Wi-Fi, the local wireless link is the problem. You do not need to understand every light — just note whether the internet light changes at the same time as your drop.

The Quick Restart, Done Properly

A restart fixes a surprising number of drops, but only if you do it in the right order and give it enough time. Unplug the power cable from both the modem and the router, if they are separate boxes. If you have a single combined box, unplug that one box. Wait a full 30 seconds — not two seconds, not a quick flick of a switch. This lets the memory clear and the connection fully reset.

Plug the modem back in first and wait until its lights settle, usually two to three minutes. Then plug the router back in and wait another two minutes. Then reconnect your device. A rushed restart, where you unplug and immediately replug, often does nothing because the hardware never fully powered down.

Router Placement and Overheating

Where the router sits matters more than most people expect. Wi-Fi signals weaken through walls, floors, metal, water, and large appliances. A router hidden inside a cupboard, behind a television, on the floor, or next to a fish tank will struggle. The best spot is usually central in your home, out in the open, at roughly chest to head height, away from thick walls and metal objects.

Move the router if you can, even by a metre or two, and test again. Small moves can make a big difference if the signal was previously blocked by a metal filing cabinet or a brick chimney breast. Do not put the router directly next to a microwave, a baby monitor, or a cordless phone base station, all of which can interfere with Wi-Fi. Keep it away from direct heat sources such as radiators, and make sure its vents are not blocked by books or fabric.

Overheating is a real cause of drops, especially for older routers in warm rooms or enclosed spaces. If the router feels very hot to the touch, or if drops get worse in the evening when the heating is on, ventilation may be the issue. Move it into open air, keep it upright as designed, and do not stack other electronics on top of it. If it still overheats in open air, the hardware may be failing.

Update Firmware and Check for Provider Outages

Outdated router firmware can cause drops, security problems, and incompatibility with newer phones and laptops. Log in to your router's admin page — the address is usually printed on the label, often something like 192.168.1.1 or 192.168.0.1 — and look for a firmware update or software update section. If an update is available, install it, and let the router restart fully. Do this when you do not urgently need the internet, because the update will take the connection offline for a few minutes.

While you are in the admin page, note your Wi-Fi settings but do not change several things at once. Change one setting, test for a while, and only then change another. If you change five settings at once and the problem goes away, you will never know which one fixed it — and you may have broken something else without realising.

Separate the 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz Bands

Most modern routers broadcast two Wi-Fi bands: 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz. The 2.4 GHz band travels further through walls and is better for distance, but it is slower and more crowded. The 5 GHz band is faster and less crowded, but it does not travel as far and struggles through thick walls and floors. Many routers combine both under a single network name and let devices choose automatically, which sounds convenient but can cause drops if a device keeps hopping between bands at the edge of coverage.

If your router lets you split the bands into two network names — for example, adding "5G" to one name — try it as a test. Connect a device that drops near the router to the 5 GHz network, and connect a distant device to the 2.4 GHz network, and see whether the drops stop. You can always merge them again later. This is not a permanent commitment; it is a diagnostic step.

As a worked example, imagine a laptop in a bedroom two rooms from the router that drops every twenty minutes. On a combined network, it may be clinging to a weak 5 GHz signal instead of switching to the stronger 2.4 GHz signal. Splitting the bands and connecting that laptop to 2.4 GHz only may stop the drops entirely, at the cost of slightly lower top speed. That trade-off is worth it: a stable slower connection beats a fast connection that vanishes mid-call.

If your router does not offer a split option, or your provider locks the settings, try moving closer to the router for a test period. If the drops stop when you are near the router, distance and band-steering are likely involved, and a Wi-Fi extender or mesh system — covered later — may be the right fix.

Interference, Neighbours, and Channel Congestion

Wi-Fi shares radio space with neighbours, Bluetooth devices, microwaves, and other electronics. In flats and terraced houses, dozens of nearby networks can crowd the same channels, especially on 2.4 GHz, which has only a few non-overlapping channels. The result can be drops, slow speeds, and lag, even when the signal bars look full.

Most routers have an "auto channel" setting that is supposed to pick the least crowded channel. Sometimes it works well; sometimes it picks badly or never changes. In your router's wireless settings, look for the channel option. If it is set to auto and you still have drops, try manually selecting a different channel as a test — on 2.4 GHz, channels 1, 6, and 11 are the usual non-overlapping choices. On 5 GHz there are more channels available and congestion is usually less of a problem.

Fix the Device That Keeps Dropping

If only one device drops, work through that device before replacing the router. Start with the simplest steps: forget the network on that device and reconnect, entering the password fresh. This clears a corrupted saved profile, which is a common cause after a router password change or a software update. On a phone, go to Wi-Fi settings, tap the network, choose Forget, then reconnect. On a computer, remove the known network and add it again.

Next, check for software updates on that device — operating system updates, and on a computer, Wi-Fi driver updates. An outdated or buggy Wi-Fi driver is a classic cause of drops on Windows laptops in particular. Check the laptop manufacturer's support page for your exact model rather than relying only on automatic updates, which sometimes miss driver updates.

Power-saving settings are another hidden culprit. Laptops and phones try to save battery by turning down or switching off the Wi-Fi adapter when idle. On a Windows laptop, look in Device Manager under Network adapters, open your Wi-Fi adapter's properties, and check the Power Management tab: uncheck any option that lets the computer turn off the device to save power, as a test. On a phone, check whether a battery-saver mode is aggressively managing Wi-Fi, and test with it off.

Also check for VPNs, security software, and third-party Wi-Fi managers. A VPN that disconnects and reconnects can look like a Wi-Fi drop. Temporarily disable the VPN and see whether the drops stop. If they do, the VPN, not your Wi-Fi, needs attention. Similarly, if drops started right after installing new security software, test with it temporarily disabled, following that software's own instructions.

When Distance Is the Real Problem

Sometimes the router is fine, the device is fine, and the house is simply too big or too solidly built for one router. Signs include: drops only in a far room or garden office, full signal near the router but one bar at the far end, and a connection that works for browsing but fails on video calls from that room. In that case, no settings tweak will fully fix it — you need to extend coverage.

The options, in rough order of reliability, are: a wired Ethernet connection, a mesh Wi-Fi system, a wired access point, and a plug-in extender. Ethernet is the most reliable by far, and if you can run a cable or use existing wiring, it will beat any wireless fix. A mesh system, with two or three units placed around the home, is the best wireless option for larger homes because devices roam between units more smoothly than with a basic extender. A basic extender can help, but it can also halve your speed and create a second network that devices cling to badly, so place it halfway between the router and the dead zone, not inside the dead zone itself.

As a worked example, consider a home office at the end of a garden. A single router in the house will rarely cover it reliably, however good the settings. A mesh unit in the room nearest the garden, or a wired access point in the office, is a structural fix, not a settings tweak. Spending money here makes sense only after you have confirmed that distance is the cause — for example, by testing the same laptop next to the router for an hour without drops.

Common Mistakes That Make Drops Worse

A few well-meaning moves tend to make things worse. The first is factory-resetting the router without noting the settings, as mentioned above. The second is changing many settings at once, which makes diagnosis impossible. The third is buying a new router before testing whether the old one is actually at fault — a new router in the same bad spot, with the same interference, will drop just the same.

Another mistake is hiding the router to make it look tidy, inside a closed cabinet or behind heavy furniture, and then wondering why the signal is weak. Wi-Fi needs air and line of sight as much as possible. Similarly, stacking the router under a pile of other hot electronics, or placing it on top of a radiator, invites overheating drops.

Finally, do not ignore the simplest explanation: a failing power supply. If the router reboots itself, or its lights flicker, try a different compatible power adapter if you have one, or ask your provider for a replacement. Routers do wear out, typically after several years of continuous use, and a router that drops more and more often despite every fix may simply be at the end of its life.

If This Does Not Work, Try This

Work through this order if the drops persist. First, test wired versus wireless, and one device versus every device, to confirm where the problem lives. If it is wired too, contact your provider and report a line fault, noting the times of drops and whether the internet light changes.

If it is wireless only, restart properly, improve placement and ventilation, update firmware, and split the bands as a test. If one device still drops, forget and reconnect the network, update its drivers and system, disable aggressive power saving, and test without a VPN.

If distance is the cause, test near the router, then consider a mesh system or a wired connection for the far room. If nothing helps and the router is old, hot, or rebooting on its own, ask your provider about a replacement, or consider replacing a very old privately owned router. Keep notes of what you tried and what changed — support teams can help much faster when you can say, "Wired is stable, wireless drops every twenty minutes in the bedroom, and splitting the bands did not help," rather than just, "The internet is broken."

Questions People Ask

Why does my Wi-Fi drop at the same time every day?

A regular time pattern often points to interference or load. It could be a neighbour's network getting busy, a microwave or other appliance on a timer, a scheduled backup or update saturating the connection, or your provider doing maintenance. Note the exact time for a few days and check whether it lines up with anything in your home. If it lines up with nothing at home, ask your provider whether there is scheduled work.

Why does my phone work but my laptop keeps dropping?

Different devices have different Wi-Fi adapters, antennas, and software. A laptop with an older adapter or a buggy driver may struggle on a network that a newer phone handles easily. Work through the device-specific steps above — forget and reconnect, update drivers, and check power settings — before blaming the router.

Should I buy a Wi-Fi extender or a mesh system?

For a single small dead spot, a well-placed extender may be enough. For a larger home, multiple dead spots, or a need for smooth roaming between rooms, a mesh system is usually better, though it costs more. Either way, confirm distance is the cause first by testing near the router, and place any extra unit halfway to the dead zone, not inside it.
