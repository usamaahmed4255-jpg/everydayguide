How to Unclog a Drain Without Calling a Plumber

A slow or blocked drain is usually fixable in 30 minutes. Work through these methods in order — plunger, drain snake, trap — and know when to stop and call a plumber.

A slow drain is one of those problems that gets worse the longer you ignore it, and most of them are fixable without a plumber. The trick is working through the methods in order, from the simplest to the most involved, and stopping at the point where a professional is genuinely needed. This guide covers sinks and bathtub drains; toilets are a different job with a different plunger, covered briefly at the end.

Start by identifying which drain is affected and how badly. A drain that is slow but still draining is usually a partial blockage near the surface. A drain that does not drain at all, with standing water, is a fuller blockage, and you should avoid running more water into it while you work. A drain that backs up when another fixture is used suggests a deeper problem, and that is a stopping point — see the safety rules below before you start.

Before you start: two safety rules

Never mix drain cleaners. Chemical drain cleaners are caustic, and mixing two different products — or using a chemical cleaner after a plunger has splashed it around — can cause burns and toxic fumes. This guide does not use chemical drain cleaners at all, because the mechanical methods below work better and do not damage pipes. If you have already poured a chemical cleaner down the drain, do not plunge it, and wear gloves and eye protection for everything that follows.

Know when to stop. Stop and call a plumber if: more than one drain is blocked at the same time (that suggests a main line problem, not a single clog); water is coming up in a different fixture when you plunge; you smell sewage; or you have old, fragile pipes and something feels stuck. Those are not DIY territory.

Gather your tools before you start, so you are not leaving a half-dismantled sink to search for a bucket. You will need: rubber gloves, a cup plunger, old towels, a bucket, a flathead screwdriver, and, for later steps, a hand drain snake and channel-lock pliers. Eye protection is sensible if you have already used a chemical cleaner, or if you are plunging a drain with standing water that may splash. Keep a phone nearby in case you need to call a plumber mid-job — that is not failure, it is good planning.

Step 1: Remove the obvious blockage

Start at the surface, because the most common clogs are shallow. Pull out the sink stopper or the bathtub drain cover (most unscrew or lift out; a flathead screwdriver helps with stiff ones) and look inside. Hair, soap scum, and small objects collect right at the top, and a surprising number of "blocked" drains are fixed by pulling out a clump of hair with gloved fingers or needle-nose pliers. Clean the stopper itself too — coated stoppers drain slowly even when the pipe is clear.

For bathroom sinks, also check the pop-up mechanism: the rod underneath the sink that lifts the stopper can collect hair and gunk. Unscrew the pivot nut, pull the rod out, clean it, and reassemble. It takes five minutes and fixes slow bathroom sinks more often than any product.

For bathtubs, the drain cover may be held by screws, or it may lift out with a twist. If you have a trip-lever drain, the stopper mechanism extends into the pipe and can collect hair along its length — pulling it out carefully and cleaning it often clears a slow tub drain completely. Take a photo before you disassemble anything, so reassembly is straightforward. That one habit saves more time than any tool, especially for mechanisms you have never seen before.

After clearing the surface, run water for thirty seconds and watch how it drains. If it drains normally, you are done — clean up, replace the cover, and note what caused it for prevention later. If it is still slow, move to step two. Do not skip ahead to the snake or the trap while a surface blockage may still be contributing; each step builds on the last, and a shallow clog can mask a deeper one.

Step 2: Plunge it properly

Most people plunge wrong, which is why they conclude plunging does not work. Technique matters:

  1. Use a cup plunger (the simple rubber cup shape) for sinks and tubs. A flange plunger (with the extra rubber flap) is for toilets — the wrong shape will not seal.
  2. Block the overflow outlet. Sinks and tubs have an overflow hole or slot near the top; water and air escape through it and kill your pressure. Plug it with a wet cloth and hold it in place.
  3. Make sure there is enough water in the basin to cover the plunger cup — the seal needs water, not air.
  4. Push down firmly, then pull up sharply, about 15–20 times, keeping the cup sealed to the drain. You are trying to dislodge the clog with pressure changes, not to force it down with one heroic push.
  5. Lift the plunger suddenly on the last pull and see if the water drains. If it does, run hot water for a minute to flush the loosened debris.

If the water level does not change after a few solid rounds, move on — more plunging will not fix a clog that is further down the pipe.

Two details make plunging work better. First, the seal: if the cup does not seal flat against the drain, because of a curved basin or a damaged cup, pressure escapes and nothing happens. A thin smear of petroleum jelly around the cup rim can improve the seal on an older plunger, and a new cup plunger costs little if yours is cracked or hardened. Second, the overflow: on a double sink, block the other drain as well, or pressure escapes through it instead of reaching the clog. Ask someone to hold the cloth over the overflow if you need both hands for plunging — this is a two-person job done well, and a one-person struggle done badly.

If plunging moves water but the drain slows again within a day, the clog is probably only partially dislodged, or there is build-up further down that plunging alone cannot clear. Move to the snake rather than plunging repeatedly for days. Repeated plunging on a stubborn clog can also stress old pipe joints, so treat it as a diagnostic step with a limit, not as an endless remedy.

Step 3: Try a drain snake (hand auger)

A drain snake is a long, flexible metal cable with a corkscrew end, sold at hardware stores for roughly $10–25. It reaches clogs that a plunger cannot, and it is the tool plumbers reach for first in most cases.

  1. Feed the cable slowly into the drain, rotating the handle as you go. Do not force it — if it stops, rotate and gently push; forcing can scratch pipes or get the snake stuck.
  2. When you feel resistance, you have likely reached the clog. Rotate the cable to catch the material, then pull it back out slowly. Expect something unpleasant — hair and soap scum, usually.
  3. Repeat once or twice, then flush with hot water for a couple of minutes.

Clean the snake thoroughly afterwards (hot water and disinfectant, outside if possible) and dry it before storing, or it will rust.

Step 4: Check the trap (under-sink, for sinks only)

If the clog is in a sink and the snake did not reach it, the trap — the U-shaped (or P-shaped) bend under the sink — is the next stop, and it is where most kitchen and bathroom sink clogs actually live. This sounds intimidating and is genuinely straightforward:

  1. Put a bucket under the trap. Water will come out — that is expected, and the bucket is why it does not matter.
  2. Unscrew the slip nuts on either side of the trap by hand (channel-lock pliers help if they are stiff, but do not overtighten on reassembly — hand-tight plus a quarter turn is enough).
  3. Lower the trap into the bucket and empty it. Clear out whatever is inside, rinse it in the bucket, and check the short pipe sections on either side.
  4. Reassemble in the same order, run water, and check for drips at the joints. A slow drip means a nut needs a small extra turn, or the washer inside is worn and needs replacing (a few cents at a hardware store).

If the trap is clear and the drain is still blocked, the clog is further down the line — that is plumber territory, not a reason to disassemble more pipework.

Before you unscrew anything, clear the area under the sink and lay towels down. The water in the trap is dirty, and it will spill despite the bucket if you tilt the trap awkwardly. Work slowly, keep the trap upright as you lower it, and empty it into the bucket rather than into the sink. Check the washers as you go: a cracked or flattened washer is a common cause of a slow drip after reassembly, and replacing it now is far easier than discovering the drip later and dismantling everything again.

If the slip nuts will not budge by hand and pliers only round them off, stop. Old plastic nuts can crack, and old metal ones can seize. A cracked nut turns a simple trap clean into a plumbing repair, and that is a point where calling a plumber is cheaper than continuing. Similarly, if the pipes under your sink are corroded, leaking already, or visibly fragile, do not dismantle them — describe what you see to a plumber instead.

What about baking soda and vinegar?

You will see this advice everywhere, and it is mostly theatre. Baking soda and vinegar fizz impressively, but the fizz is carbon dioxide escaping up the drain, not force pushing a clog out — and the resulting mixture is mostly water. It can freshen a drain's smell slightly, and it will not damage anything, but it does not clear a real blockage. If a drain is genuinely clogged, skip it and use the plunger or the snake. If a drain is just a bit slow and smelly, a kettle of hot (not boiling) water followed by a flush is more useful, and free.

One genuine exception: never pour boiling water into a porcelain sink or toilet, or into PVC pipes that are already stressed — thermal shock can crack porcelain. Hot tap water is fine; a rolling-boil kettle is not necessary.

The same caution applies to shop-bought chemical cleaners, even though this guide does not use them. They can damage older pipes, harm septic systems, and make later mechanical work dangerous if they splash back. If you have already used one and the drain is still blocked, tell the plumber you call — they need to know, for their own safety, before they plunge or dismantle anything.

Preventing the next clog

Most clogs are repeatable, which means they are preventable:

  • Bathroom drains: A mesh drain cover (a few dollars) catches hair before it enters the pipe. Clean it weekly — thirty seconds with a tissue.
  • Kitchen sinks: Never pour cooking grease down the drain. It solidifies in the pipes and traps everything else. Pour it into a jar, let it solidify, and bin it. Wipe greasy pans with a paper towel before washing.
  • All drains: Once a month, flush with hot tap water for a minute. It does not dissolve everything, but it moves along the soap scum and residue before they build into a clog.

Prevention also means knowing what should never go down a drain: coffee grounds, rice and pasta (which swell), fibrous vegetables, wipes labelled "flushable" (they do not break down like toilet paper), and sanitary products. A drain is not a bin, and the most effective prevention is simply keeping solids out of it in the first place. In the kitchen, a sink strainer that catches food particles, emptied into the bin after washing up, prevents a large share of kitchen clogs on its own.

A note on toilets

Toilets need a flange plunger (the one with the extra rubber ring that seals into the bowl outlet) and a slightly different technique: gentle first push to expel air and seat the seal, then vigorous plunging. Never use a drain snake meant for sinks in a toilet — it can scratch the porcelain. If a toilet clog survives proper plunging, or if the water level rises when you flush other fixtures, call a plumber rather than improvising.

Toilet clogs are also the ones where knowing what was flushed matters. If a child has flushed a toy, or if wipes or sanitary products were flushed, say so to the plumber — it changes the approach. And if a toilet repeatedly clogs without an obvious cause, the problem may be low water flow, a partial blockage further down, or a venting issue, none of which are fixed by more plunging.

Most single-fixture clogs give way at step one, two, or three. Work in order, keep the chemical cleaners in the shop, and know the stopping points — that is the whole skill, and it saves a plumber's call-out fee most of the time.

Questions people ask

Why does my drain keep clogging in the same place? Because the cause has not changed. A bathroom drain that clogs with hair will clog again unless a drain cover catches the hair. A kitchen drain that clogs with grease will clog again unless grease stops going down it. Fix the habit, not just the clog, or you will repeat this guide every few months.

Is it safe to use a plunger if I have a septic system? Yes, plunging and snaking are safe for septic systems — they are mechanical, not chemical. Chemical drain cleaners are the concern for septic systems, because they can kill the bacteria the system relies on. Stick to mechanical methods, and keep grease and solids out of the drain.

How do I know if it is a main line problem? The signs are: more than one drain blocked at once, water backing up into a different fixture (for example, the tub fills when the toilet flushes), sewage smells, or gurgling from drains when another fixture is used. Any of those means the blockage is beyond a single fixture, and a plumber with proper equipment should handle it.

Can I use a wet vacuum to clear a drain? Sometimes, for a shallow blockage, a wet vacuum can suck out standing water and debris — but it is messy, and it does not reach deeper clogs. It is useful for clearing water so you can work, not as a replacement for plunging or snaking. If you try it, use a vacuum rated for wet use, and expect a mess.
