How to Pack for a Week-Long Trip in a Carry-On

Seven days of clothes in one carry-on is doable. Use the capsule method, a real packing list, and the liquids rules for the US and UK — no checked bag needed.

A week-long trip does not require a checked bag. It requires a system: fewer, better-chosen clothes that all work together, packed in a way that uses every inch, and toiletries that pass security without a second thought. This guide gives you that system, with a packing list you can reuse for almost any week-long trip.

The system works because it removes decisions. Instead of packing for every possible occasion, you pack a small set of pieces that combine into many outfits, plus a plan for washing halfway through if needed. That is the difference between a carry-on that closes easily and a suitcase that needs sitting on. It also means you travel lighter, move faster through airports, and never wait at a baggage carousel wondering whether your bag made the connection.

The capsule: 5 tops, 2 bottoms, 1 extra layer

The core idea is simple: every top must go with every bottom. If an item only works with one other item, it does not earn its place. For seven days, in a temperate climate, pack:

  • 5 tops: a mix of T-shirts and one smarter shirt or blouse. Choose a colour palette of two neutrals (black, white, navy, grey) plus one accent colour, so everything coordinates without thinking.
  • 2 bottoms: one pair of trousers or jeans, one skirt or second pair of trousers — whichever you actually wear. Dark colours hide wear between washes.
  • 1 extra layer: a jumper, cardigan, or light jacket, depending on the destination's evenings.
  • Underwear and socks: 7 sets, or 4 sets plus a plan to wash halfway through (a sink wash with a travel soap sheet takes ten minutes and dries overnight in most climates).
  • 1 pair of shoes worn, 1 pair packed, maximum. Shoes are the biggest space cost in any bag. Wear the bulkier pair (trainers or boots) and pack the lighter pair (flats or sandals). Stuff socks and small items inside the packed shoes — dead space is wasted space.
  • Sleepwear: one set, lightweight.
  • Accessories: one scarf or belt that works with multiple outfits, sunglasses, and any jewellery you will actually wear — not the whole collection.

That is roughly seven outfits from eight main pieces, because the pieces mix. If your trip includes one formal evening, swap one top for the formal item rather than adding a whole extra outfit — one item, worn once, is the most expensive thing in any bag.

Choosing the palette before you choose individual items is what makes the capsule work. Lay the candidate pieces on a bed and check: does each top work with both bottoms? Does the extra layer work over every top? If a piece only works with one other piece, it fails the test, however much you like it. That discipline feels strict when packing, but it pays off every morning of the trip, when getting dressed takes two minutes instead of a deliberation over mismatched clothes.

For different climates, adjust the capsule rather than abandoning it. In hot weather, lighter fabrics and shorter sleeves, but the same counts. In cold weather, the extra layer becomes a warmer coat worn on the plane, and the tops become layers rather than standalone outfits — thermal base layers pack small and add warmth without bulk. In rainy destinations, a packable rain jacket worn on travel days earns its place far more than an umbrella, which is bulky and easily forgotten.

How to fold it: rolling vs. folding, honestly

The internet argues about this endlessly. The honest answer: rolling saves space for soft, casual clothes (T-shirts, knitwear, underwear) and reduces creasing for those fabrics. Structured items (shirts, blazers, smart trousers) fold better and crease less when folded properly. Use both: roll the soft stuff, fold the structured stuff, and put the folded items on top where they will not be compressed by everything else.

Packing cubes help more than any folding method. They compress soft clothes, keep categories separate (tops in one, underwear in another), and mean you can pull out one cube in a hotel room without unpacking the whole bag. They are not essential, but for a week in a carry-on they are the single best purchase — a basic set costs less than a checked-bag fee on most airlines.

Pack in this order: heavy items (shoes, toiletries bag) at the bottom near the wheels, rolled clothes in the middle, folded items and anything you need on arrival on top. Put your nightwear and a fresh top on top — after a long journey, you want those without emptying the bag.

If you hate packing cubes, compression bags are an alternative, but be careful: they can compress clothes so tightly that creasing worsens, and they make it tempting to overpack a bag that then exceeds weight limits. Cubes organise without over-compressing, which is why they suit a week-long carry-on better. Whatever you use, pack the bag once, close it, lift it, and walk around with it for a minute before travel day. A bag that is hard to lift or that bursts open when set down is a bag that needs editing, not forcing shut.

Toiletries and the liquids rules (US and UK)

The liquids rules are nearly identical in the US and UK, with one difference in how they are checked, so learn one set:

  • Each container must be 100 ml (3.4 oz) or less.
  • All containers must fit in one transparent, resealable bag of about 1 litre (1 quart).
  • One bag per passenger, presented separately at security.

In the UK, some airports now allow containers up to 2 litres with new CT scanners — but not all airports, and not all lanes, so the 100 ml rule remains the safe assumption until your specific airport says otherwise. In the US, the TSA 3-1-1 rule (3.4 oz bottle, 1 quart bag, 1 bag per traveller) is unchanged.

Practical tips that save hassle:

  • Buy solid alternatives where you can: shampoo bars, solid deodorant, and soap bars do not count as liquids at all, and they last longer than travel bottles.
  • Decant, do not buy travel sizes. Small refillable bottles cost less over time than miniature versions of your usual products, and you get the products you actually use.
  • Put the liquids bag at the very top of your carry-on, in an outside pocket if you have one. Fumbling for it at security while a queue builds behind you is avoidable stress.
  • Medications are exempt from the liquids limit in both the US and UK, but keep them in original packaging with the prescription label, and carry a copy of the prescription for anything controlled.

Decanting deserves a little care: label each small bottle, fill it the day before travel (not in a rush at the door), and test that the caps seal. A leaking shampoo bottle inside a carry-on ruins clothes far more effectively than any packing mistake. Put the liquids bag itself inside a second zip bag if you are worried about leaks, so a spill is contained. And remember that gels, creams, and pastes count as liquids — toothpaste, moisturiser, and hair gel all go in the bag, even though they do not feel like liquids in the usual sense.

What goes in your personal item (not the carry-on)

Your "personal item" — a backpack, tote, or small bag that goes under the seat — is free extra space, and it should hold everything you need during the journey plus anything you cannot afford to lose:

  • Passport/ID, tickets, phone, charger, and a power bank.
  • Wallet, keys, and any medication.
  • A change of top and essential toiletries (toothbrush, a small deodorant) — if your carry-on is gate-checked on a full flight, you still have the basics.
  • Entertainment: book, headphones, downloaded shows. Do not rely on plane Wi-Fi.
  • A snack and a water bottle (empty through security, filled afterwards). Airport food is expensive and slow.

Never put passports, medication, electronics, or valuables in a bag you might have to hand over at the gate.

Gate-checking is the scenario most carry-on packers forget. On a full flight, overhead bins fill up and later boarders may be asked to gate-check their carry-on. If that happens, you want your essentials already in your personal item, not buried in the bag being taken away. Pack the personal item as if the carry-on might disappear for a day — because, occasionally, it does, and the travellers who cope best are the ones who planned for it.

Adapting the system: work, beach, and cold trips

A work trip changes the capsule, not the method. Swap one casual top for a smarter shirt, pack one pair of smart trousers as one of your two bottoms, and make the extra layer a blazer or smart jacket that works over every top. Keep the same counts; change the formality. A single pair of smart shoes worn on travel days, plus a lighter pair packed, still fits the one-worn-one-packed rule.

A beach trip is the opposite adjustment: swimwear counts as a top for daytime, cover-ups double as layers, and sandals replace one shoe pair. The risk on a beach trip is overpacking "just in case" resort wear that never gets worn. Apply the same test: every piece must pair with at least two others, or it stays home.

A cold trip tests the capsule most, because warm clothes are bulky. The answer is layers, not bulk: thermal base layers, a mid-layer, and a warm outer layer worn on the plane, rather than three thick jumpers packed in the bag. Wear the coat, wear the boots, and pack the lighter layers. The bag still closes, and you are warm on arrival without having unpacked anything.

The night-before check

Run through this the evening before you leave, not at the door:

  1. Bag weight and size: Weigh the packed bag (most airlines allow 7–10 kg for carry-on, but it varies — check your airline's exact limit) and confirm it fits the airline's size gauge. A bag that fits one airline may not fit another.
  2. Chargers and adapters: Phone charger, plus a plug adapter if travelling internationally. One multi-port charger beats three single ones.
  3. Documents: Passport validity (many countries require six months beyond your trip), tickets, accommodation confirmation, travel insurance details — saved offline on your phone as well as on paper.
  4. Home: Bins out, perishables dealt with, a light on a timer if you use one, someone holding a spare key if the trip is a week.

Do the check the evening before for a reason: anything you discover missing can still be fixed that evening, whereas a discovery at the door means either leaving without it or missing your transport. Charge devices overnight, download entertainment and offline maps, and put travel documents in the personal item, not in the carry-on's side pocket where they are easy to forget when the bag is gate-checked.

A reusable packing list

Copy this, adjust for climate and occasion, and use it every time:

  • 5 tops (palette: 2 neutrals + 1 accent)
  • 2 bottoms, 1 extra layer
  • 7 underwear, 7 socks (or 4 + wash plan)
  • Shoes: 1 worn, 1 packed
  • Sleepwear, 1 scarf/belt, sunglasses
  • Toiletries bag (100 ml rule, solids where possible)
  • Personal item: documents, electronics, chargers, medication, snack, water bottle, book/headphones
  • Phone charger + plug adapter

Seven days, one carry-on, no checked-bag queue, no lost luggage risk, and a bag you can carry up a flight of stairs without regret. The system works because it is a system — fewer choices, made once, reused every trip.

Questions people ask

What if I need more than a week of clothes? Wash halfway through. A sink wash of underwear, socks, and a couple of tops, dried overnight, effectively doubles the capsule. Laundries and hotel laundry services are also options on longer trips, but a simple wash plan is usually enough to extend a carry-on system to ten days or more without adding a bag.

Do packing cubes really help, or are they just marketing? They help with organisation more than with raw space. Cubes keep categories separate, compress soft clothes modestly, and make unpacking and repacking far quicker. If you dislike them, a simple system of rolling and grouping by category in zip bags achieves much of the same effect. The benefit is organisation, not magic.

What if my airline's carry-on size is smaller than my bag? Check the airline's exact limits before you buy a bag, and before each trip if you fly different airlines. A bag that fits one airline may not fit another, and budget airlines in particular tend to be stricter. If your bag is too large, either pack lighter in a smaller bag or accept a checked bag for that trip — forcing an oversized bag into a sizer at the gate is stressful and can incur a fee.

Can I bring food through security? Solid food is generally allowed in both the US and UK, though rules vary by destination and by item, especially for fresh produce and meat products when crossing borders. Pack snacks in your personal item, keep them simple and sealed, and check your destination's import rules before bringing food across a border. Liquids and gels in food, like yoghurt or soup, count towards the liquids limit.
