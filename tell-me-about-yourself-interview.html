How to Answer "Tell Me About Yourself" in an Interview

The first interview question trips up more candidates than any other. Use the Present–Past–Future structure, plus examples for students, career changers, and experienced hires.

"Tell me about yourself" is almost always the first interview question, and it is the one most candidates answer badly. Not because they lack experience, but because they treat it as small talk and ramble through their life story. Interviewers are not asking for your biography. They are asking for a short, confident summary of why you are sitting in that chair. Here is a structure that works, and examples for three very different candidates.

The first thing to accept is that this question is not really about you in general. It is about you in relation to this job. Everything you say should help the interviewer see the connection between your background and the role on the table. That filter — "does this help them see the fit?" — is the simplest way to decide what to include and what to leave out. Your childhood, your hobbies in detail, and your full employment history all fail that filter, however interesting they are elsewhere.

What the interviewer actually wants

The question does three jobs at once. It warms you up, it checks whether you can organise your thoughts, and it tells the interviewer which parts of your background to dig into next. A good answer is 60 to 90 seconds long — roughly 150 to 200 words — and it ends at a point that connects to this job. A five-minute answer that starts with childhood fails all three jobs. A one-line answer ("I'm a hard worker") fails them too.

The rule: professional, present to past to future, and always landing on this role.

Notice what a good answer does for the interviewer. It gives them a map: they know your current situation, they know which past achievements to ask about, and they know why you want this job. A rambling answer gives them no map, so their follow-up questions wander too. A structured answer steers the rest of the interview towards your strengths, because the interviewer will naturally pick up the threads you offered. That is why preparation for this one question pays off far beyond its ninety seconds.

The Present–Past–Future structure

This is the simplest reliable structure, and it fits almost every candidate:

  1. Present: What you do now, in one or two sentences. Your current role, study, or situation, plus a strength that matters for this job.
  2. Past: Two or three highlights from what came before — chosen because they relate to this job, not because they are your whole history. One achievement with a number is worth more than a list of duties.
  3. Future: Why this role, specifically, is the next step. Name something real about the job or the company — the work itself, the team, the direction it is going — not generic praise.

Then stop. Ending cleanly matters as much as starting well. A simple "And that's why I'm excited about this role" is a good landing. Do not trail off with "...so, yeah."

When choosing your past highlights, work backwards from the job description. Circle the two or three skills the posting emphasises, then find the best evidence you have for each. That evidence becomes your past section. Anything else, however proud of it you are, stays out of this answer — you can bring it up later if a question calls for it. Selectivity is not dishonesty; it is the whole skill of a good interview answer.

Example 1: A recent graduate with little experience

> "I'm a recent business graduate, and over the past year I've focused on building practical skills alongside my degree — I ran the marketing for our university enterprise society, growing its Instagram following from 200 to 1,400, and I completed a three-month internship in a small company's operations team, where I helped reorganise their stock system. What I enjoyed most was the operations side — fixing messy processes and seeing the difference quickly. That's what drew me to this role: it's hands-on operations work in a growing team, and it's the direction I want to build my career in."

Why it works: no apology for being new, two concrete pieces of evidence, and a future that names the actual job.

Notice also what it does not do. It does not list every module studied, it does not apologise for lack of experience, and it does not claim skills it cannot show. The numbers are modest and believable — a society account grown from 200 to 1,400, a three-month internship — and that believability is part of why it works. Interviewers trust specific, modest claims far more than grand, vague ones.

Example 2: A career changer

> "I've spent the last six years in retail management, most recently running a team of twelve in a busy high-street store. The part of that job I was best at — and enjoyed most — was training new starters and improving how the team handled busy periods; we cut customer complaints by about a third over eighteen months. I'm looking to move into customer success because it uses those same skills — teaching, problem-solving, and keeping people happy under pressure — in a field I want to grow in long term. This role stood out because it combines onboarding new clients with ongoing support, which is exactly where my experience sits."

Why it works: it reframes the old career as relevant instead of apologising for it, and it explains the change as a deliberate move, not an escape.

Career changers often make the opposite mistake: they spend the answer explaining why they are leaving, which puts the old job centre stage. This example spends one sentence on the old role's scope, then moves to what was enjoyed and done well, then to the bridge into the new field. The future section names the specific combination in this role — onboarding plus ongoing support — which shows the candidate has read the posting rather than sending the same answer everywhere.

Example 3: An experienced hire

> "I'm a project coordinator with five years in construction supply, currently managing delivery schedules for three active sites. In my current role I took over a supplier process that was running two weeks late on average and got it to on-time delivery within a quarter, mainly by rebuilding the tracking sheet and holding a short weekly check-in with each supplier. I'm looking for a step up into a role with more ownership of the full delivery cycle, which is why this position appealed to me — it covers planning through to handover, not just one slice of it."

Why it works: present role, one sharp achievement with numbers, future tied to the specific scope of the new job.

For experienced candidates, the trap is the opposite of the graduate's: too much to say, so the answer becomes a tour of an entire career. This example resists that by choosing one achievement that proves the skill the new role needs — ownership of a process, end to end — and letting the rest of the CV speak for the rest. If the interviewer wants more, they will ask, and then you can expand. The opening answer is a trailer, not the whole film.

Adapting it to your situation

  • No experience at all: Use school, volunteering, or projects as your "past" — the structure still works. See the CV guide on this site for how to turn everyday life into evidence.
  • A career gap: Do not hide it and do not over-explain it. One calm sentence ("I took two years out to care for a family member, and during that time I kept my skills current with...") and move on to the future. Interviewers respect brevity here far more than a defensive speech.
  • Overqualified for the role: Emphasise the part of the work you genuinely want to do, not the seniority you are leaving behind. "I'm looking to get back to hands-on work with clients" lands better than "I just need something for now."

Two more situations come up often. If you are applying for a role in a new industry, make the bridge explicit: name the skill that transfers, give one example of it in your old context, and say why you want to apply it here. If you have moved jobs frequently, do not spend the answer justifying each move; pick the thread that connects them — a skill, a sector, a type of work — and present the moves as steps along that thread. The interviewer will ask about individual moves if they care; your opening answer does not need to pre-empt every question.

Five mistakes to avoid

  1. Reciting your CV from the top. They have read it. Summarise and select; do not repeat.
  2. Starting with personal life. Hobbies and family come later, if at all. The first 30 seconds should be professional.
  3. Being vague. "I'm a people person" says nothing. One example says everything.
  4. Badmouthing a previous employer. Even if justified, it makes interviewers wonder what you will say about them.
  5. Ending with an apology. Never close with "I know I don't have much experience, but..." End on the future and the fit.

A sixth mistake is worth adding: memorising the answer word for word. A word-perfect recital sounds stiff, and if nerves make you forget a line, you can freeze completely. Learn the three beats — present, past, future — and the key facts inside each, but let the exact wording vary. That way a stumble costs you a sentence, not the whole answer.

How to practise without sounding rehearsed

Write your answer out once, using the structure above, then practise it aloud three or four times — not to memorise it word for word, but until the three beats (present, past, future) feel natural. Then practise it once more while doing something else, like walking or washing up. If you can deliver it while distracted, you can deliver it under interview nerves.

Record yourself on your phone and listen back once. You will hear the filler words, the rushed middle, and the trailing ending — and fixing those three things is usually all the polish the answer needs.

Timing matters, so practise with a clock. If your answer runs past two minutes, cut a past highlight rather than speaking faster. Speaking faster under nerves makes you harder to follow, not more impressive. Aim for a pace you would use explaining something to a colleague, with a brief pause before the future section — that pause signals that you are landing the answer, and it gives the interviewer a moment to register the connection to their role.

Practise the opening line most of all. The first sentence is where nerves hit hardest, and a confident start carries the rest. "I'm a..." followed by your current role or study is enough. You do not need a clever opening; you need a clear one.

The interviewer will form an impression in the first two minutes. A clear, 90-second answer to this one question sets the tone for everything that follows. It is worth an hour of your preparation time, because almost every interview starts exactly here.

Questions people ask

What if the interviewer asks this in a video interview? The structure is identical. Look at the camera when you deliver the future section, so the landing feels directed at them rather than at your notes. Keep notes out of sight but nearby if you need a prompt for a number — glancing down once is fine, reading aloud is not.

Should I mention a hobby or personal interest? Only if it genuinely connects to the role — for example, a coding side project for a technical role, or coaching for a training role. Otherwise save personal interests for later questions, or for the closing small talk. The opening answer should stay professional.

How do I handle it if I have no relevant past at all? Use the closest evidence you have, and be honest about the step you are taking. School, volunteering, and personal projects all count when you describe what you did and what it shows. A short, honest answer with one real example beats a long answer padded with vague claims.

What if I go blank mid-answer? Pause, breathe, and jump to the next beat. "And looking ahead..." is a perfectly natural bridge back on track, and interviewers barely notice a brief pause. What they do notice is panicked backtracking, so do not try to recover a forgotten sentence word for word — move forward instead.
