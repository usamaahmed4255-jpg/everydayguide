How to Write a Cover Letter for Your First Job (With a Worked Example)

No experience does not mean nothing to say. A short, specific cover letter beats a long generic one — here is the structure that works.

What a Cover Letter Is For

A cover letter is a short message that accompanies your CV or application. It is not a repeat of your CV, and it is not your life story. Its job is simple: explain, in your own words, why you want this particular job, why you are a plausible fit despite limited experience, and why the hiring manager should read your CV. For a first job, that is an important job, because your CV alone may look thin. The letter gives context that a list of qualifications cannot.

Hiring managers often skim cover letters quickly, especially for entry-level roles with many applicants. A short, specific letter that mentions the role, the company, and one or two relevant strengths will stand out more than a long, generic letter that could apply to any job. Aim for one page at most, and ideally three to five short paragraphs. If the job posting says a cover letter is optional, sending a good one still helps. If it says not to send one, follow that instruction.

Before You Write: Research the Role

Do not start writing until you have read the job posting twice and looked at the company's website. Note the exact job title, the team or department if given, and the main duties. Underline the skills and qualities the posting asks for — for example, reliability, communication, teamwork, basic IT skills, or a willingness to learn. Those words are your guide to what to emphasise.

Then look for one specific thing about the company that genuinely interests you. It could be the service they provide, a local branch you have visited, a product you use, or a value they state on their website. You do not need to flatter the company at length. One honest, specific sentence shows you have done more than send the same letter everywhere.

Also check the practical details: who to address the letter to, if a name is given, and how to submit it. If no name is given, "Dear Hiring Manager" is a safe, professional opening. Avoid outdated openings such as "To whom it may concern" if you can, and never guess a person's gender or title from a first name you are unsure about. If the posting gives a name, use it exactly as written.

As a worked example, imagine applying for a part-time retail assistant role. The posting asks for friendly customer service, reliability, and weekend availability. A quick look at the store's website shows it prides itself on helpful, unhurried service. Those details — friendly service, reliability, weekend availability — become the backbone of your letter. You are not inventing experience; you are matching what you can honestly offer to what the employer asked for.

The Structure That Works

A reliable structure for a first-job cover letter has four parts: opening, why this job, why you, and closing. Keep each part short.

The opening should state the role you are applying for and where you saw it, in one or two sentences. For example: "I am applying for the Retail Assistant role advertised on your website, and I would welcome the opportunity to join your team." That is clear and professional. Do not open with a long personal history or an apology for lack of experience. Lead with the role, not with what you lack.

The second part, why this job, is one or two sentences about why this role and this company appeal to you. Be specific. Mentioning the company's service, location, or reputation shows interest. Avoid generic lines such as "I have always dreamed of working for a successful company," which say nothing about this job in particular.

The third part, why you, is the longest section, usually one or two paragraphs. This is where you connect your experience — school, college, volunteering, part-time work, projects, clubs, or caring responsibilities — to the skills in the posting. You do not need paid work experience to do this. Organising a school event shows planning. Helping in a family shop shows customer contact. A group project shows teamwork. Choose two or three strengths that match the posting, and give a brief example for each. One concrete example beats a list of adjectives.

The closing should thank the reader, restate your enthusiasm briefly, and state what you would like next — usually an interview. Keep it to two or three sentences. Include your phone number and email address if they are not already on your CV or application form, and sign off professionally: "Yours sincerely" if you addressed a named person, "Yours faithfully" if you used "Dear Hiring Manager," or simply "Kind regards" in a less formal online application, following the tone of the posting.

Writing About Experience You Do Not Have Yet

The hardest part of a first cover letter is the feeling that you have nothing to write about. Reframe it: employers hiring for a first job do not expect a long work history. They expect evidence of reliability, willingness to learn, and basic skills. Your task is to show those qualities through whatever you have done.

School and college work counts. Completing coursework on time, working in a group, presenting to a class, or learning a software tool are all relevant, if you link them to the job. Volunteering counts, even for a short period. Helping at a community event, coaching younger children, or supporting a charity shop shows commitment and people skills. Hobbies can count if they are relevant — for example, running a small online shop shows initiative, or captaining a sports team shows leadership — but do not pad the letter with unrelated hobbies just to fill space.

Be honest and specific. Instead of writing "I am a hard worker and a team player," write what you did: "As part of a group project, I coordinated our timetable and kept the team on track to meet the deadline." The second version lets the reader judge the skill for themselves. Avoid exaggeration. Claiming expert skills you do not have will become obvious at interview, and it damages trust.

Do not apologise repeatedly for lack of experience. One brief acknowledgement, if needed, is enough — for example, "Although this would be my first paid role, I have developed relevant skills through..." — and then move on to what you can offer. The letter should leave the reader thinking about your strengths, not your gaps.

A Worked Example

The following is a worked example only, written for an illustrative retail assistant role. Adapt the structure and tone, not the details, and replace every example with your own true information.

Dear Hiring Manager,

I am applying for the part-time Retail Assistant role advertised on your website. I visit your store regularly and admire the friendly, unhurried service your team provides, and I would welcome the chance to contribute to that standard.

Although this would be my first paid job, I have developed relevant skills through school and volunteering. At school, I worked in a team of four on a charity fundraising project, where I helped plan the event, keep track of donations, and speak to visitors on the day. The project raised money for a local food bank and taught me how to stay organised under time pressure. I also volunteer once a week at a community centre, where I welcome visitors, answer questions, and help set up activities. That role has given me confidence talking to people I have not met before and handling busy periods calmly.

I am reliable, available at weekends, and keen to learn. I understand the role involves helping customers, keeping displays tidy, and working as part of a small team, and I am ready to take on those responsibilities and learn your systems quickly. I take pride in doing a task properly, whether that is a school assignment or a shift at the community centre.

Thank you for considering my application. I would welcome the opportunity to discuss the role at interview, and I am available at your convenience. My contact details are on my CV.

Yours faithfully, Alex Morgan

Notice what this example does: it names the role, gives a specific reason for interest, offers two concrete examples linked to the posting, states availability, and closes politely. It is short, it does not claim experience the writer does not have, and it does not repeat the CV. Your letter should do the same for your role, in your own words.

Tailor It Every Time

A cover letter that is clearly copied and pasted, with only the company name changed, is easy to spot. Hiring managers notice when the letter mentions the wrong role, the wrong company, or skills that do not match the posting. Tailoring does not mean rewriting the whole letter from scratch each time. It means adjusting the opening, the reason for interest, and the examples so they fit this specific job.

Keep a master version with your best paragraphs, then adapt it per application. Change the job title, the company name, and the specific detail that attracted you. Swap examples so the two or three strengths you highlight are the ones this posting asks for. If one role emphasises IT skills and another emphasises customer service, the letter should reflect that difference, even if the rest stays similar.

Proofread the tailored version as if it were new, because most errors happen during adaptation: a leftover company name from a previous application, a wrong job title, or a sentence that no longer makes sense after editing. Reading the letter aloud catches awkward phrasing and errors that silent reading misses. Ask someone you trust to read it once, if you can — a fresh pair of eyes often spots what you have stopped seeing.

Common Mistakes That Weaken a First Cover Letter

The most common mistake is making the letter too long. A two-page cover letter for an entry-level role suggests you cannot prioritise information. Keep it to one page, with short paragraphs and white space. If you are struggling to cut it down, remove adjectives and general claims first, and keep concrete examples.

Another mistake is repeating the CV word for word. The CV lists qualifications and dates; the letter should explain and connect them. If a sentence could be copied straight from your CV without adding meaning, rewrite it or delete it.

Generic flattery also weakens a letter. Lines such as "Your company is the best in the industry and I have always wanted to work there" sound insincere unless you can back them with a specific reason. One honest detail beats a paragraph of praise.

Spelling and grammar errors matter, especially for roles involving writing or customer communication. Use spellcheck, but do not rely on it alone, because it will not catch a correctly spelled wrong word, such as "their" instead of "there." Check names, job titles, and company names character by character. A misspelled company name suggests the letter was not checked.

Finally, do not use informal language, slang, or humour that could be misread. A cover letter is a professional document, even for a casual workplace. Warm and polite is right; overly casual or jokey is risky when you have not met the reader yet.

Formatting and Sending

Formatting should be clean and simple. Use a standard font, a readable size, and consistent spacing. Put your name and contact details at the top, followed by the date, and then the employer's name and address if you have them. If you are applying through an online form, paste the letter into the cover letter box and check that paragraph breaks survived the paste — online forms sometimes collapse formatting.

Save the file with a clear name, such as "Alex-Morgan-Cover-Letter.pdf," if you are uploading a document, rather than "letter-final-v3.pdf." If the posting asks for a specific format, follow it exactly. If it does not specify, PDF is usually safest because it preserves formatting across devices.

Before sending, run a final checklist: Is the job title correct? Is the company name spelled correctly? Is the hiring manager's name correct, if used? Does the letter mention why this job, why you, and what you would like next? Is it one page or less? Have you read it aloud once? If you can answer yes to all of those, send it. Do not delay an application for days seeking perfection; a good, tailored letter sent on time beats a perfect letter sent after the closing date.

If This Does Not Work, Try This

If you are sending tailored letters and hearing nothing back, do not assume the letter alone is the problem. Check the whole application: Is your CV clear and tailored too? Are you meeting the essential requirements in the posting, such as location, availability, or a required qualification? Are you applying for roles at the right level? A first-job search often takes many applications, and silence is common even for strong candidates.

Ask for feedback where possible. Some employers will give brief feedback if you ask politely after a rejection, especially smaller employers. Use that feedback to adjust your next letters. You can also ask a careers adviser, teacher, or trusted adult to review one letter and one CV together, because the two documents should complement each other, not duplicate each other.

If a particular role matters to you, consider whether there is a legitimate extra step: visiting the workplace, if appropriate, or mentioning a relevant project or volunteering experience more prominently. Do not chase an employer repeatedly or send the same letter again unchanged. One polite follow-up after a reasonable interval, if the posting invites it, is enough.

Questions People Ask

Do I need a cover letter if the job posting says it is optional?

It is usually worth sending one, if you can write a good, tailored letter. An optional cover letter is a chance to show interest and context that a CV alone does not provide. If you cannot tailor it and would only send a generic letter, it is better to send no letter than a careless one.

How long should a cover letter for a first job be?

Aim for one page, typically three to five short paragraphs, roughly 250 to 400 words as an illustrative range. That is enough to state the role, explain your interest, give two or three examples, and close politely. Longer letters are rarely read in full for entry-level roles.

What if I have no work experience at all?

Use school, college, volunteering, projects, clubs, caring responsibilities, and relevant hobbies, linked to the skills in the posting. Employers hiring for first jobs expect this. Focus on what you did, not just adjectives about yourself, and be honest about the scale of each example.

Should I mention salary or availability in a cover letter?

Mention availability only if it is relevant to the role, such as weekend availability for retail or shift patterns. Do not mention salary expectations unless the posting specifically asks for them. If it does ask, research the typical range for the role and location first, and state your expectation briefly and professionally.

Is it okay to use a template?

A template for structure is fine; a template filled with generic sentences is not. Use a structure like the one in this guide, but write the content in your own words, tailored to each role. Hiring managers can tell when a letter has not been adapted, and an unadapted letter suggests low interest in that specific job.
