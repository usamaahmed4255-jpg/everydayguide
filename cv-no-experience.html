How to Write a CV With No Experience (With a Template Outline)

No work history? No problem. Learn how to build a strong CV from school, volunteering, and everyday skills — with a template outline and example lines.

Every job posting seems to ask for experience, and every first job seems to require it. It is a frustrating loop, but it is not airtight. Employers hiring for entry-level roles know you are starting out; what they are really looking for is evidence that you are reliable, willing to learn, and able to do the basics well. A CV built from school, volunteering, projects, and everyday responsibilities can show all of that. Here is how to build one, section by section.

Before you write a word, change how you think about "experience". Employers do not only mean paid jobs. They mean: has this person been trusted with something, finished something, dealt with people, or handled a responsibility? If you have done any of those things — at school, at home, in a club, in a community — you have material. The CV's job is to present that material in the language employers recognise: what you did, where, when, and what came of it.

A quick note on CV vs résumé

In the UK, Ireland, and most of Europe, the document is called a CV. In the US and Canada, the same document is usually called a résumé, and it is typically kept to one page. The structure in this guide works for both — if you are applying in the US, call it a résumé and trim harder; if you are applying in the UK, "CV" is expected and two pages is acceptable, though one strong page still beats two padded ones. This guide uses "CV" throughout, but everything applies either way.

There is one more US/UK difference worth knowing. In the US, it is normal to leave personal details like date of birth, marital status, and a photo off a résumé. In the UK, a photo is also not expected, and those personal details are unnecessary. In both cases, give your name, a professional email address, a phone number, and your city — not your full home address. That is enough for an employer to contact you, and it keeps private details private.

Start with a short personal profile — not an objective

The top of your CV should be a profile of three to four lines: who you are, what you are aiming at, and two strengths with a hint of evidence. Skip the old-fashioned objective ("seeking a challenging position where I can grow") — it says nothing.

Weak: "Hardworking and motivated individual looking for a job where I can use my skills."

Stronger: "Recent school leaver with strong written English and two years of weekend retail volunteering. Organised, comfortable with customers, and keen to build a career in administration."

Notice the difference: the second version names something real (volunteering), names the target (administration), and gives the reader a reason to keep reading. Write yours last, after the rest of the CV is done, so you know what to highlight.

A good test for a profile is whether it would still make sense if you swapped in another applicant's name. If it would — "hardworking, motivated, team player" — it is generic, and it should be rewritten with specifics only you could claim. Name the role you want, name one or two things you have actually done, and name a strength that follows from them. For example, a profile for a warehouse role should sound different from a profile for a customer service role, even if both are written by the same person. Tailor the profile to the job in front of you, every time.

Education: list it properly, then add what you actually did

List your most recent education first: school or college name, dates, and qualifications with grades where they are good. But do not stop at a bare list. Under each, add one or two lines about work that relates to the job:

  • A presentation you gave, a group project you led, or a piece of coursework with a real outcome ("Researched and presented a local history project to a class of 30").
  • Relevant subjects, named specifically, not "various subjects".
  • Any short courses, online certificates, or training — first aid, food hygiene, a coding intro course — with the provider and date.

If your grades are not your strong point, keep the list brief and let the rest of the CV do the talking. Nobody hires on grades alone for entry-level roles, and a CV that hides weak grades behind a wall of padding looks worse than one that moves on quickly.

If you are still studying, say so plainly, with an expected completion date: for example, "Currently studying — expected June 2027". Employers would rather see an honest "in progress" than a qualification that looks finished when it is not. Similarly, if you left education without the qualifications you hoped for, do not invent grades. List what you did complete, add any short courses since, and let the experience section carry the weight. A CV that is honest about one weak area and strong elsewhere is far more convincing than one that unravels at the first background check.

Turn everyday life into experience

This is the section that makes or breaks a no-experience CV. "Experience" does not only mean paid jobs. Anything where you were trusted, organised something, helped people, or handled money counts — if you describe it with specifics.

Volunteering: Charity shop shifts, community events, coaching a kids' team, helping at a place of worship. Describe what you actually did: "Served customers, handled cash, and restocked displays during Saturday shifts at a charity shop" beats "Volunteering, 2024–2025".

School and college roles: Prefect, team captain, society organiser, student representative. "Organised a 40-person fundraising event that raised £350 for new sports equipment" is a real achievement, even if it happened at school.

Personal projects: A blog you kept up for six months, a small online shop, videos you edited, a community group you run. Name the thing, say what you did, and give a number where you can: followers, posts, sales, events.

Family responsibilities: Caring for siblings, helping run a family business, translating for relatives. These show reliability and maturity. Describe them plainly and briefly — one or two lines, not a paragraph of explanation.

Part-time and casual work counts double: Babysitting, lawn mowing, tutoring a neighbour's child, delivery work. If money changed hands and someone relied on you, it is work experience. Say so.

For each item, use the same shape: role or activity, organisation or context, dates, then two bullet points starting with a verb. "Helped", "organised", "served", "handled", and "ran" are stronger than "was responsible for" or "involved in". And add a number or an outcome wherever you honestly can: how many people, how much money, how often, how long. Numbers are not boasting; they are the difference between a claim and evidence. If you cannot give a number, give a concrete detail instead — what kind of customers, what kind of tasks, what system you used.

Be honest about scale. "Ran a small online shop selling handmade cards, 20 orders in six months" is credible and interesting. Inflating it into a business empire is not, and it will collapse under one interview question. Employers hiring for first jobs expect small numbers. They are looking for reliability and initiative, not a track record that rivals a manager's.

Skills: show them, do not just list them

A skills section that reads "communication, teamwork, Microsoft Office, time management" tells the reader nothing, because every CV says it. Instead, attach each skill to a piece of evidence:

  • "Customer service: two years of weekend volunteering serving the public in a busy charity shop."
  • "Written English: school newsletter contributor; wrote and edited three issues."
  • "IT: confident with Word, Google Docs, and spreadsheets; built a simple budget tracker for a family business."

Three or four evidenced skills beat ten bare adjectives. If you claim a language or a software tool, be honest about your level — "conversational Spanish" and "basic Excel" are credible; "fluent" and "expert" will be tested.

Choose skills that match the job description. Read the posting, circle the skills it asks for, and check which ones you can evidence. If a posting asks for cash handling and you handled a till at a charity shop, that goes at the top. If it asks for teamwork and you played in a team sport for three years, that is your evidence — but say what your role in the team was, not just that you were on it. A skills section that mirrors the posting, with proof for each claim, reads as a deliberate application rather than a generic document sent everywhere.

A template outline you can copy


[Your name]
[Phone number] | [Email address] | [City, not full address]

PROFILE
Three to four lines: who you are, target role, two evidenced
strengths.

EDUCATION
[Most recent first: institution, dates, qualifications]
- One line on relevant project or coursework

EXPERIENCE (paid, voluntary, or project-based)
[Role / activity] — [Organisation or context], [dates]
- What you did, with a number or outcome
- What you did, with a number or outcome

SKILLS
- Skill: evidence
- Skill: evidence
- Skill: evidence

INTERESTS (optional, one line)
One genuine interest that adds something — a sport you play
regularly, a craft, a language you are learning. Skip "reading
and travelling" unless you can make it specific.

Keep it to one page if you can, two at most. Use a clean font, consistent dates, and no coloured headings or graphics — hiring software reads simple formatting best, and a human reader trusts it more.

Formatting details matter more than people expect. Use the same date format throughout (for example, "June 2024 – August 2025"), the same bullet style, and headings that are clearly headings. Do not use tables, text boxes, or images to lay out a CV — applicant tracking software often reads them badly, scrambling the order or dropping text. A plain, well-spaced document in a standard font is not boring; it is readable, by machine and by person alike.

Common mistakes to avoid

First, lying or exaggerating. Employers check references and ask follow-up questions, and a single invented claim can end an application that was otherwise going well. Second, sending the identical CV to every job. A CV tailored to the role — profile rewritten, most relevant experience first, skills matched to the posting — consistently outperforms a generic one sent to fifty jobs. Third, including irrelevant history at length. A Saturday job from three years ago deserves two lines, not half a page, if it has nothing to do with the role you want now.

Fourth, neglecting the email address and phone number. An unprofessional email address from school, or a voicemail greeting that sounds like a joke, can undo a good CV before an interview is even offered. Create a simple firstname.lastname address for job hunting, and check that your voicemail is clear and your phone is charged on days you expect calls. Fifth, leaving unexplained gaps that look like contradictions. Gaps are fine — studying, caring, travelling, looking for work — but dates that overlap impossibly or jump without explanation raise questions. Keep dates accurate, and be ready to explain a gap calmly in an interview.

Before you send it: the five-minute check

  1. Read it aloud. Anything that sounds like a slogan ("dynamic self-starter") should be replaced with a fact.
  2. Check every date. Gaps are fine; contradictions are not.
  3. Make sure your email address is professional — ideally firstname.lastname, not a nickname from school.
  4. Save and send as PDF unless the posting asks for Word. PDFs keep your formatting intact on every device.
  5. Tailor the profile and the order of your experience to each role. A CV tweaked for the actual job beats a generic CV sent to fifty jobs.

Add a sixth check: open the PDF yourself, on a phone, before sending it. That is how many recruiters will first see it. If the text is tiny, the layout breaks, or a page is blank, fix it now. And keep a master copy of your CV with everything in it, then save a tailored copy per application. That way tailoring never means deleting your only record of a role you may want to restore later.

You do not need experience to have a good CV. You need evidence — and you almost certainly have more of it than you think.

Questions people ask

Should I include a photo on my CV? In the US and UK, no — it is not expected, and it adds nothing about your ability to do the job. The exception is roles where a portfolio or appearance is genuinely part of the work, such as acting or modelling, where the application instructions will say so. For office, retail, warehouse, and most other entry-level roles, leave it off.

What if I truly have nothing to put under experience? Almost everyone has something, once everyday life counts: school projects, helping family, a club, a sport, a short course. If you genuinely have nothing yet, a short period of volunteering — even a few Saturday shifts — gives you real material quickly, and it shows initiative in itself. Do that first, then write the CV around it.

How long should a no-experience CV be? One page is ideal, two pages at most. If yours runs to two pages, check whether the second page is earning its place or just repeating the first. A short, evidenced CV is stronger than a long, padded one, because the reader can see the evidence without wading through filler.

Do I need a cover letter as well? If the posting asks for one, yes. If it does not, a short cover note or email explaining why you want this specific role still helps, especially for a first job. Keep it to a few paragraphs: the role, why it fits you, and one or two pieces of evidence. It should complement the CV, not repeat it word for word.
